## From a mechanism story to a tested claim

"This peptide binds a receptor" is a narrower claim than "this product helps people." The first can be investigated in structural or laboratory studies. The second needs human outcome evidence for the **specific product and population**. Our [mechanism primer](/guide/how-do-peptides-work) explains the basic receptor sequence; here we examine what each type of experiment can establish, using GLP-1 receptor research as an example.

**Evidence grade: moderate for this evidence-chain example.** The cited primary studies test different links in the chain. Their findings should not be transferred to unrelated peptides.

## What does each experiment test?

| Evidence | What the cited study observed | What remains unanswered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | A cryo-electron microscopy study resolved semaglutide bound to the GLP-1 receptor and G protein ([Zhang et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260945/)). | An image of engagement does not measure a patient's outcome. |
| Cell signaling | Experiments in pancreatic β-cells and islets measured cAMP and insulin release and used a receptor antagonist to probe GLP-1 receptor involvement ([Shuai et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33369112/)). | These GLP-1 receptor experiments did not test a clinical semaglutide outcome. |
| Molecule design | The semaglutide discovery study examined receptor potency, enzymatic stability, and albumin binding ([Lau et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26308095/)). | Laboratory properties do not establish the size of a benefit or harm in people. |
| Human outcomes | STEP 1 randomized adults with overweight or obesity **without diabetes** to semaglutide or placebo, both with lifestyle intervention, and measured weight and adverse events over 68 weeks ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/)). | One trial does not establish the same outcome for another molecule, population, or use. |

The β-cell experiment was about GLP-1 receptor signaling and used GLP-1 and glucagon to probe that pathway. It should not be described as a semaglutide-specific cell assay ([Shuai et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33369112/)). Likewise, STEP 1 tested clinical outcomes; its result does not prove that any single molecular step fully explains those outcomes ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/)).

## A practical way to read the next claim

First, identify the **exact peptide and formulation**. Then check whether the cited experiment measured binding, a downstream signal, exposure, or an outcome in people. Note its comparison group and study population. A claim should stay within the question its source actually tested. For example, STEP 1 supports conclusions about its randomized population and studied intervention, while the structural study supports a conclusion about a receptor complex ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/); [Zhang et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260945/)).

This distinction matters when a page uses "mechanism" as a shortcut for effectiveness. Mechanistic plausibility can motivate a trial; it cannot replace one. See the [semaglutide dossier](/peptides/semaglutide) for the product-specific evidence context.

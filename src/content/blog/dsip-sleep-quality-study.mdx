Delta sleep-inducing peptide (DSIP) is widely marketed as a sleep aid that works with the body's own sleep mechanisms rather than sedating the brain. That framing rests on a very thin human evidence base: two small controlled trials, both more than thirty years old, with the more informative of the two reporting only weak effects.

For safety considerations and broader research context on DSIP, see our [DSIP safety profile](/safety/dsip-safety/) and [peptide evidence levels guide](/guides/peptide-evidence-levels-explained/).

## What We Know

### The human trials

Searching the published literature for controlled DSIP trials in insomnia returns two studies.

**Schneider-Helmert, Graf and Schoenenberger (1981), *The Lancet*.** A short randomized clinical trial report titled "Synthetic delta-sleep-inducing peptide improves sleep in insomniacs." It is indexed as a letter with no abstract, so the design details are not retrievable from the PubMed record.

**Bes, Schulz and colleagues (1992), *Neuropsychobiology*.** A double-blind, matched-pairs, parallel-group randomized trial in **16 chronic insomniac patients** [dsip-clinical-trial]. Participants received 25 nmol/kg DSIP **intravenously** across five consecutive laboratory nights (night 1 adaptation, night 2 baseline, nights 3-5 treatment).

### What the 1992 trial found

The results were modest and mixed [dsip-clinical-trial]:

- Sleep efficiency was higher with DSIP than placebo
- Sleep latency was shorter with DSIP than placebo
- One subjective tiredness measure decreased in the DSIP group
- **No other measure changed**, including subjective sleep quality

The authors judged the statistically significant differences to be weak and potentially attributable to changes in the placebo group rather than to a true treatment effect. Their conclusion was explicit: short-term treatment of chronic insomnia with DSIP is not likely to be of major therapeutic benefit.

### What is not established

There is no published controlled trial supporting any of the following, all of which circulate in marketing material:

- A specific percentage increase in slow-wave sleep
- A defined improvement in sleep efficiency over multi-week dosing
- Intranasal or subcutaneous DSIP efficacy in insomnia
- Absence of next-day impairment measured against an active comparator
- Any effect on dependence liability or tolerance over time

The 1992 trial used intravenous dosing over three treatment nights in sixteen people. That is the ceiling of what the controlled human record supports.

### Mechanism of Action

DSIP is a nine-amino-acid peptide that modulates sleep through several mechanisms [sleep-peptide-review]:

**Neurotransmitter modulation:** DSIP influences GABAergic and serotonergic signaling without directly binding GABA-A receptors like benzodiazepines and Z-drugs.

**Cortisol regulation:** The peptide appears to normalize cortisol rhythms, which are often dysregulated in insomnia.

**Circadian influence:** DSIP may enhance natural circadian sleep signals rather than overriding wakefulness.

**Delta wave enhancement:** The peptide specifically promotes delta-frequency brain activity associated with restorative deep sleep.

Unlike sedative-hypnotics that produce generalized CNS depression, DSIP appears to work with physiological sleep mechanisms.

## What It Means

### Clinical Implications

The appeal of a non-sedating sleep agent is real. Many sleep medications cause next-day drowsiness, cognitive impairment and accident risk, and benzodiazepines and Z-drugs often suppress slow-wave and REM sleep. A peptide that promoted physiological sleep without those trade-offs would be genuinely useful.

DSIP has not been shown to be that agent. The trial record does not include next-day cognitive testing against an active comparator, does not report dependence or tolerance outcomes, and does not extend beyond a handful of nights.

### Comparison to Current Treatments

| Medication | Sleep Onset | Deep Sleep | Next-Day Effects | Dependence |
|------------|-------------|------------|------------------|------------|
| Benzodiazepines | Improved | Suppressed | Common | Yes |
| Z-drugs | Improved | Variable | Common | Lower |
| Suvorexant | Improved | Maintained | Rare | Minimal |
| DSIP | Shorter latency in one small trial | Not established | Not studied | Not studied |

### Limitations

The limitations here are not caveats on a promising result — they are the reason there is no result to build on:

**Study duration:** Three treatment nights in the 1992 trial. Nothing longer has been published.

**Sample size:** Sixteen participants. Far too few to detect anything but a large effect.

**Delivery route:** Intravenous. The intranasal and injectable products sold to consumers have never been tested in a controlled insomnia trial.

**Age of the evidence:** The most recent controlled human sleep trial of DSIP was published in 1992.

## What's Next

### Development Path

There is no active clinical development programme for DSIP in insomnia. Establishing whether the peptide does anything useful would require, at minimum [insomnia-treatment-update]:

**A modern controlled trial:** Adequately powered, with polysomnography and a validated subjective endpoint.

**Formulation work:** Evidence that any consumer route of administration delivers the peptide at all.

**Safety studies:** Extended-duration data on tolerance and dependence, neither of which has been assessed.

**Mechanism studies:** Additional research to fully characterize how DSIP promotes natural sleep.

### Regulatory Considerations

DSIP faces a complex regulatory path:

**Peptide classification:** As a synthetic version of an endogenous peptide, regulatory classification requires clarification.

**Approval pathway:** Novel mechanism may require new clinical endpoints and outcome measures.

**Safety database:** Substantial safety data will be required given chronic use patterns in insomnia.

### Research Peptide Context

DSIP has been available as a research peptide for years, with variable quality and limited clinical data [sleep-peptide-review]. Research peptides from unregulated sources may differ in purity, potency, and safety from material used in clinical trials — and in DSIP's case there is barely any clinical trial material to compare against.

The honest summary is that DSIP is an interesting endogenous peptide whose clinical sleep evidence stalled in the early 1990s, after the investigators who tested it concluded it was unlikely to help. Claims of specific percentage gains in deep sleep or sleep efficiency do not come from any published controlled trial.

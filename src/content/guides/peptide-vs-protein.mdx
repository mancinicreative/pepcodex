## Start with the bond

Amino acids can join through peptide bonds. A molecule containing amino-acid residues joined this way is a **peptide** under [IUPAC-IUB nomenclature](https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/AminoAcid/A1113.html). The terms below describe the chain, not a medical use or a level of evidence.

| Term | What it describes |
| --- | --- |
| Amino acid | A building block that can be joined to other amino acids. |
| Peptide | Two or more amino-acid residues joined by peptide bonds. |
| Oligopeptide | A comparatively short peptide chain. |
| Polypeptide | A longer peptide chain. |
| Protein | A term commonly used for longer chains that function as biological molecules. |

## Is there a fixed peptide–protein cutoff?

No. IUPAC-IUB nomenclature says a polypeptide of more than **about 50 residues** is usually called a protein, while authors differ greatly on where they begin using that word. That is a naming convention, not a test of function, safety, or clinical benefit. A residue count alone does not describe a molecule's full structure or a formulated product.

The terms can overlap. A protein contains amino-acid chains, and a chain called a polypeptide in one context may be discussed as a protein in another. Use the source's definition when comparing studies rather than forcing every molecule into a strict 50-residue table.

## What the term leaves unanswered

The molecule's name does not tell you how a medicine was made or delivered. Reviews of peptide therapeutics describe varied structures, chemistry, and development approaches ([Muttenthaler et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33536635/); [Lau and Dunn, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28720325/)). The term also does not establish a product's effect in humans.

For an applied example of how to check a medical claim, read [what a peptide or protein label can tell you](/blog/peptide-vs-protein). That article examines molecule identity, formulation, human evidence, and product labeling. This guide is limited to the vocabulary.

faqs:
  - question: What is Cardiogen?
    answer: >-
      Cardiogen is a synthetic tetrapeptide, Ala-Glu-Asp-Arg (AEDR), from
      Vladimir Khavinson's Russian "bioregulator" peptide program. Khavinson's
      group patented the sequence as a substance for restoring myocardium
      function (US7662789, EP1758923). It is sold as a research chemical.
  - question: Is Cardiogen the same as Cartalax?
    answer: >-
      No, and this is a common mix-up worth being precise about. Cartalax is the
      tripeptide Ala-Glu-Asp (AED), a chondroprotector. Cardiogen is the
      tetrapeptide Ala-Glu-Asp-Arg (AEDR) — one extra arginine residue. They are
      different molecules with different molecular weights (333.29 Da versus
      489.5 Da) and separate literatures, so a study of AED says nothing about
      Cardiogen.
  - question: Is Cardiogen FDA approved?
    answer: >-
      No. Cardiogen is not approved by the FDA or any major regulatory agency
      for any use, and it has never been evaluated in a registered human
      clinical trial. A patent is not an approval — it establishes a claim over
      the molecule, not that the molecule works.
  - question: What does the research on Cardiogen actually show?
    answer: >-
      All of it is preclinical and nearly all of it is Russian. A 2022 Khavinson
      review reports that AEDR reduced mortality about threefold after
      experimental myocardial infarction in rats. A 2012 study found the
      tetrapeptide raised cytoskeletal and nuclear-matrix protein synthesis in
      cultured mouse fibroblasts. A 2006 study applied Cardiogen to organotypic
      heart explants from young and aged rats. There are no human trials and no
      independent Western replication of the cardiac findings.
  - question: Does Cardiogen treat or protect the heart?
    answer: >-
      That is not established. The cardioprotective claim rests on animal
      infarct work reported in Russian secondary literature and on ex-vivo
      explant culture — not on any human trial. Cardiogen should never be used
      in place of evidence-based cardiac care.
  - question: How does Cardiogen relate to other Khavinson peptides?
    answer: >-
      Cardiogen is one of Khavinson's tissue-labelled short peptides (others
      include Vesugen/KED, Pinealon/EDR, Livagen/KEDA and Cartalax/AED). The
      family shares a proposed mechanism — cell penetration and chromatin
      binding — and shares its central weakness: almost all of the evidence
      comes from a single research school and lacks independent clinical
      validation.

Cardiogen is the tetrapeptide **Ala-Glu-Asp-Arg (AEDR)**, one of the tissue-labelled short peptides from Vladimir Khavinson's Russian bioregulator programme. Khavinson's group patented the sequence specifically as a "peptide substance restoring myocardium function," and the published preclinical work does sit in cardiac territory — but every one of those studies comes from the same research school, and none of it is a human trial.

**Key context:** the strongest claim attached to Cardiogen — roughly a threefold reduction in mortality after coronary artery ligation in rats — reaches us through a 2022 review by Khavinson and colleagues rather than through a primary paper we have been able to locate in PubMed. That matters. A review by the originating group restating its own unpublished-in-English result is weaker evidence than the confident phrasing suggests, and readers should treat the figure as unverified rather than as a replicated finding.

### Important Limitations

- The single most-cited result (post-infarct mortality in rats) is reported in review, not in a primary paper we could locate
- No human studies of any kind and no randomized controlled trials; not approved by any regulator
- The only located study applying Cardiogen directly to cardiac tissue is a 2006 organotypic rat-heart explant assay, published in Russian
- Effectively all evidence originates from the Saint Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology; there is no independent Western replication
- The mechanistic chain is assembled from separate systems — plant histones, mouse fibroblasts, rat explants — with no study joining them
- Pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and any clinical effect in humans are unknown
- Should never replace evidence-based cardiac care

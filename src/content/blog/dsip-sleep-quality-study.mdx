Delta sleep-inducing peptide (DSIP) is widely marketed as a sleep aid that works with the body's own sleep mechanisms rather than sedating the brain. That framing rests on a very thin human evidence base: three small controlled trials, all more than thirty years old, with the two that report retrievable results finding weak or non-significant effects.

For the full compound record see the [DSIP dossier](/peptides/dsip); for safety considerations and broader context, the [DSIP safety profile](/safety/dsip-safety) and the [peptide evidence levels guide](/guide/peptide-evidence-levels-explained).

## What We Know

### The human trials

Searching the published literature for controlled DSIP trials in insomnia returns three studies. All three were published between 1981 and 1992. All three delivered the peptide intravenously.

| Year | Journal | Design | Participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1981 [pmid-6112579] | *The Lancet* | Randomized controlled trial, indexed as a letter | Not retrievable from the record |
| 1987 [pmid-3583493] | *Int J Clin Pharmacol Res* | Double-blind crossover, four nights, polysomnography | Chronic insomniac patients |
| 1992 [dsip-clinical-trial] | *Neuropsychobiology* | Double-blind, matched-pairs, parallel-group | 16 chronic insomniac patients |

**1981, *The Lancet*.** A short randomized clinical trial report titled "Synthetic delta-sleep-inducing peptide improves sleep in insomniacs" [pmid-6112579]. It is indexed as a letter with no abstract, so the design details are not retrievable from the PubMed record. This is the study most often cited in marketing material, and it is the one whose methods nobody can inspect.

**1987, *Int J Clin Pharmacol Res*.** A double-blind crossover study with polysomnographic recording across four nights, comparing intravenous DSIP against placebo [pmid-3583493]. Nocturnal awakenings, NREM sleep latency, total waking time and waking time after sleep onset all moved in the expected direction — and **none of those differences reached significance** against either baseline or the placebo nights. Total sleep time and NREM sleep time did increase significantly versus placebo, but the increase was driven entirely by stage 2 sleep. Stage 1, slow-wave sleep (stages 3 and 4) and REM sleep were unchanged.

That last detail is worth pausing on. The compound is named for delta sleep. In the one trial that measured sleep stages directly, delta sleep did not change.

**1992, *Neuropsychobiology*.** A double-blind, matched-pairs, parallel-group randomized trial in 16 chronic insomniac patients [dsip-clinical-trial]. Participants slept five consecutive laboratory nights — night 1 adaptation, night 2 baseline, and treatment before nights 3, 4 and 5.

### What the 1992 trial found

The results were modest and mixed [dsip-clinical-trial]:

- Sleep efficiency was higher with DSIP than placebo
- Sleep latency was shorter with DSIP than placebo
- One subjective tiredness measure decreased in the DSIP group
- **No other measure changed**, including subjective sleep quality

The authors judged the statistically significant differences to be weak and potentially attributable to changes in the placebo group rather than to a true treatment effect. Their conclusion was explicit: short-term treatment of chronic insomnia with DSIP is not likely to be of major therapeutic benefit.

### What is not established

There is no published controlled trial supporting any of the following, all of which circulate in marketing material:

- A specific percentage increase in slow-wave sleep — the trial that measured it found no change
- A defined improvement in sleep efficiency over multi-week administration
- Intranasal or subcutaneous DSIP efficacy in insomnia
- Absence of next-day impairment measured against an active comparator
- Any effect on dependence liability or tolerance over time

Three treatment nights, intravenously, in sixteen people is the ceiling of what the largest controlled human record supports.

### Mechanism of Action

DSIP is a nine-amino-acid peptide, and its mechanism is less settled than most summaries imply. A 2006 review in *Journal of Neurochemistry* put the problem plainly: DSIP was isolated from rabbit cerebral venous blood in 1977 and proposed as a sleep-promoting factor, but **no DSIP gene, protein or receptor has been isolated**, and the link between the peptide and sleep has never been characterised further [pmid-16539679]. The authors describe the sleep-factor hypothesis as poorly documented and weak, and suggest the observed activity may belong to a DSIP-*like* peptide rather than DSIP itself.

Against that caveat, the mechanisms discussed in the literature are [sleep-peptide-review]:

**Neurotransmitter modulation:** DSIP is described as influencing GABAergic and serotonergic signalling without directly binding GABA-A receptors like benzodiazepines and Z-drugs.

**Cortisol regulation:** The peptide has been reported to normalise cortisol rhythms, which are often dysregulated in insomnia.

**Circadian influence:** DSIP may act on natural circadian sleep signals rather than overriding wakefulness.

Unlike sedative-hypnotics that produce generalised CNS depression, DSIP is not thought to work by suppressing arousal. Whether it works at all in humans is the open question.

## What It Means

### Clinical Implications

The appeal of a non-sedating sleep agent is real. Many sleep medications cause next-day drowsiness, cognitive impairment and accident risk, and benzodiazepines and Z-drugs often suppress slow-wave and REM sleep. A peptide that promoted physiological sleep without those trade-offs would be genuinely useful.

DSIP has not been shown to be that agent. The trial record does not include next-day cognitive testing against an active comparator, does not report dependence or tolerance outcomes, and does not extend beyond a handful of nights.

### Comparison to Current Treatments

| Medication | Sleep Onset | Deep Sleep | Next-Day Effects | Dependence |
|------------|-------------|------------|------------------|------------|
| Benzodiazepines | Improved | Suppressed | Common | Yes |
| Z-drugs | Improved | Variable | Common | Lower |
| Suvorexant | Improved | Maintained | Rare | Minimal |
| DSIP | Shorter latency in one small trial | Unchanged in the trial that measured it | Not studied | Not studied |

### Limitations

The limitations here are not caveats on a promising result — they are the reason there is no result to build on:

**Study duration:** Three treatment nights in the 1992 trial, four in the 1987 crossover. Nothing longer has been published.

**Sample size:** Sixteen participants in the largest. Far too few to detect anything but a large effect.

**Delivery route:** Intravenous in all three trials. The intranasal and injectable products sold to consumers have never been tested in a controlled insomnia trial.

**Age of the evidence:** The most recent controlled human sleep trial of DSIP was published in 1992.

**Unresolved biology:** No isolated receptor, gene or endogenous protein [pmid-16539679].

## What's Next

### Development Path

There is no active clinical development programme for DSIP in insomnia. Establishing whether the peptide does anything useful would require, at minimum:

**A modern controlled trial:** Adequately powered, with polysomnography and a validated subjective endpoint.

**Formulation work:** Evidence that any consumer route of administration delivers the peptide at all.

**Safety studies:** Extended-duration data on tolerance and dependence, neither of which has been assessed.

**Target identification:** A receptor to act on. Thirty years of failure to find one is the central obstacle [pmid-16539679].

### Where DSIP research actually continues

The peptide has not vanished from the literature — it has moved away from sleep. A 2021 study in *Molecules* reported that intranasal DSIP given before and after middle cerebral artery occlusion improved motor coordination and balance in rats, without a corresponding reduction in infarct volume [pmid-34500605]. That is a stroke-recovery model in rodents, not a sleep result, and it illustrates the pattern: contemporary DSIP work is preclinical and is largely about something other than sleep.

### Regulatory Considerations

DSIP faces a complex regulatory path:

**Peptide classification:** As a synthetic version of a putative endogenous peptide with no isolated gene, its regulatory classification is unsettled.

**Approval pathway:** A novel mechanism would require agreed clinical endpoints and outcome measures.

**Safety database:** Substantial safety data would be required given chronic use patterns in insomnia.

### Research Peptide Context

DSIP has been available as a research peptide for years, with variable quality and limited clinical data [sleep-peptide-review]. Research peptides from unregulated sources may differ in purity, potency, and safety from material used in clinical trials — and in DSIP's case there is barely any clinical trial material to compare against.

The honest summary is that DSIP is an interesting putative peptide whose clinical sleep evidence stalled in the early 1990s, after the investigators who tested it concluded it was unlikely to help. Claims of specific percentage gains in deep sleep do not come from any published controlled trial — the trial that measured slow-wave sleep found it unchanged.

Semax is a synthetic ACTH fragment approved for stroke in Russia and effectively unknown in Western medicine. This briefing summarises what the published record supports about its use in cerebral ischemia — and, more importantly, what it does not.

## What Is Semax?

Semax is a heptapeptide: the ACTH(4-7) fragment Met-Glu-His-Phe, extended at the C-terminus with the tripeptide Pro-Gly-Pro. The one-letter sequence is **MEHFPGP**, and the literature writes the molecule as ACTH(4-7)PGP [pmid-11443939]. The Pro-Gly-Pro tail is not decoration — it is the part that makes the molecule stable enough to survive intranasal delivery, and it carries measurable activity of its own [pmid-19633950].

Originally developed in Russia during the 1980s, semax has been registered there for ischemic stroke, cognitive disorders, and optic nerve disease. It remains largely absent from Western medicine because of:

- Limited publication in English-language journals
- No FDA approval, no EMA marketing authorisation, and no Western clinical trials
- Regulatory classification as a research chemical in most Western countries

For a broader overview of semax — including safety considerations, regulatory status, and other research areas — see the [semax dossier](/peptides/semax) and [semax safety profile](/safety/semax-safety). Its frequent research pairing, [selank](/peptides/selank), shares the same Pro-Gly-Pro design.

## How Semax Is Thought to Act

Three mechanisms recur across the published work, and each has been measured directly rather than inferred.

**It binds in the brain and raises BDNF.** Tritium-labelled semax showed time-dependent, specific binding to cell membranes isolated from rat basal forebrain, and intranasal application raised brain-derived neurotrophic factor protein in that region [pmid-16635254]. This is the observation most often cited as semax's core mechanism, and it is a rodent result.

**It inhibits enkephalin-degrading enzymes.** In human serum, semax inhibited the enzymes that break down enkephalins with an IC50 near 10 μM, more strongly than puromycin or bacitracin; its pentapeptide fragments retained the effect while shorter fragments did not [pmid-11443939]. Because those enzymes degrade other regulatory peptides as well, this is a plausible route by which a short peptide could have effects out of proportion to its size. It is an in-vitro finding in serum, not a demonstration of anything in a living brain.

**It reshapes the post-ischemic transcriptome.** After middle cerebral artery occlusion in rats, semax increased transcription of neurotrophins and their receptors — Bdnf, TrkC and TrkA at 3 hours, Nt-3 and Ngf at 24 hours [pmid-19633950]. Genome-wide analysis found the peptide predominantly amplified immune-system gene expression, with the effect growing between 3 and 24 hours after occlusion [pmid-24661604]. A later RNA-Seq study in the transient-occlusion model identified 394 differentially expressed genes at 24 hours, reporting suppression of inflammatory genes alongside activation of neurotransmitter genes [pmid-32580520]. At the protein level, the same model showed changes in MMP-9, c-Fos, JNK and CREB consistent with that picture [pmid-34201112].

This is a coherent mechanistic story. It is also, without exception, a rodent story.

## The Human Evidence

Two human studies are retrievable through PubMed, both from Russian clinical groups. It is worth being precise about what each one actually did.

| Study | Design | Participants | What was measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1997, *Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr* [pmid-11517472] | Controlled clinical study, acute hemispheric ischemic stroke | 30 treated, 80 conventional-therapy controls | Clinical rating scales, EEG with mapping, somatosensory evoked potentials |
| 2018, *Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr* [pmid-29798983] | Comparison across early and late rehabilitation groups | 110 post-stroke patients (43 men, 67 women, mean age 58) | Plasma BDNF, Medical Research Council motor scale, Barthel index |

The 1997 study reported faster regression of general cerebral and focal neurological deficits, motor deficits in particular, when semax was added to intensive therapy [pmid-11517472]. The 2018 study found that plasma BDNF rose and stayed elevated in the treated subgroups, whether rehabilitation began early or late [pmid-29798983].

Neither is a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Neither has been replicated by a group outside the research lineage that developed the peptide. Neither appears in international stroke neuroprotection meta-analyses. Both are published in a Russian-language journal, which is a barrier to independent scrutiny rather than a mark against the work itself.

## Limitations and Considerations

### Translational Challenges

Significant limitations apply to any preclinical neuroprotection finding:

1. **Animal model:** Rodent stroke models have poor translation to human stroke outcomes
2. **Intranasal delivery:** Nasal bioavailability varies significantly between species
3. **Timing constraints:** Pre-treatment and immediate post-stroke treatment are rarely achievable clinically
4. **Endpoint choice:** Plasma BDNF is a biomarker, not a functional outcome — a rise in it is not the same as a patient walking again

### Absence of Western Data

No randomized controlled trial of semax for stroke exists in the Western literature. Russian registration rested on studies not conducted to current international standards, which makes efficacy claims difficult to evaluate against the evidence bar regulators now apply [stroke-neuroprotection-review]. For how those tiers are weighted here, see the guide to [peptide evidence levels](/guide/peptide-evidence-levels-explained).

### Quality Control Issues

Semax obtained from research chemical suppliers varies significantly in quality:

- Purity inconsistencies
- Potential degradation products
- Unknown contaminants
- Incorrect peptide sequences

## Context in Neuroprotection Research

The history of stroke neuroprotection research is notable for failures to translate preclinical success to clinical benefit. Hundreds of agents have shown efficacy in animal models; essentially none have achieved a Western neuroprotection approval.

Potential reasons for this disconnect include:

- Species differences in cerebrovascular physiology
- Timing of treatment in trials vs. real-world stroke care
- Heterogeneity of human stroke vs. controlled animal models
- Publication bias favoring positive preclinical results

Substantial skepticism is warranted for semax given this track record. The transcriptomic work behind it is genuinely detailed, and detailed mechanism has been a poor predictor of clinical benefit in this field. Another peptide with a large indexed dossier here, [cerebrolysin](/peptides/cerebrolysin), sits in the same gap: regional-practice use without a Western neuroprotection approval.

## Research Beyond Stroke

Semax work continues outside the stroke indication. A 2025 report in *British Journal of Pharmacology* found that in a mouse spinal-cord-injury model the peptide acted on the μ opioid receptor gene *Oprm1*, affected lysosomal membrane permeabilization through deubiquitination, and improved functional recovery scores [pmid-40692165]. That is a different injury model, a different mechanism, and again a rodent — but it shows the compound is still under active investigation rather than abandoned.

## What This Means

There are plausible biological pathways through which semax could protect brain tissue during ischemic injury, the mechanistic work behind them is unusually detailed, and Russian clinical practice has treated stroke with it for decades.

However, the human record consists of two non-randomised studies from one research tradition, published in one language, never independently replicated. That is not enough to support a stroke treatment claim outside the jurisdictions that already registered it. The distance between semax's mechanistic evidence and its clinical evidence is the single most important thing to understand about this peptide.

per-tumour-type responses — together with a trial registration. The stored registration
number belongs to an unrelated study, no publication reporting those results could be
located, and the remaining sources were a company news index and two journal homepages
carrying no article identifier. Every figure attributed to that trial has been removed.
What follows is limited to peptide-drug conjugate work that can be traced to a published
paper or a registry record.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have multiple approvals and dominate the targeted-delivery
conversation. Peptide-drug conjugates (PDCs) are the smaller, less mature relative. As of
2026-08-17 no PDC carrying a cytotoxic payload has been approved, but several have completed
first-in-human testing and published the results.

## What a Peptide-Drug Conjugate Is

A PDC has three parts:

1. **Targeting peptide** — a short sequence that binds a receptor or structure enriched on
   tumour cells or tumour vasculature
2. **Linker** — chemistry intended to hold in circulation and release the payload at the target
3. **Cytotoxic payload** — an agent too toxic to give systemically on its own

The architecture mirrors an ADC, with a peptide in place of the antibody. The practical
consequences of that swap — size, penetration, manufacturing route, clearance — are the
subject of a 2026 review of targeted drug conjugates in solid tumours [conjugate-journey].

| Feature | Peptide-drug conjugate | Antibody-drug conjugate |
|---|---|---|
| Targeting moiety | Short peptide, roughly 1–3 kDa | Antibody, roughly 150 kDa |
| Tissue penetration | Favoured by small size | Constrained by size |
| Manufacturing | Chemical synthesis | Biologic production |
| Circulating half-life | Short — hours | Long — days to weeks |

The short half-life is a genuine trade-off, not a footnote. It is the reason PDC dosing
schedules differ from ADC schedules, and it is one of the reasons the class took longer to
reach the clinic. See [mechanism of action](/glossary/mechanism-of-action) for how targeting
and payload release are described in this literature.

## The PDCs With Published Human Data

Two bicyclic peptide-drug conjugates have completed first-in-human dose escalation and
published it in a peer-reviewed journal.

**[BT5528](/peptides/bt5528)** targets EphA2 and carries a monomethyl auristatin E payload.
Its first-in-human phase I dose-escalation study in patients with advanced solid tumours
associated with EphA2 expression is published [bt5528-phase1].

**[Zelenectide pevedotin](/peptides/zelenectide-pevedotin)** targets nectin-4. Its
first-in-human phase I/II dose escalation and expansion study in patients with nectin-4
expressing cancers is published [zelenectide-phase1].

Both are investigational. Neither is approved, and PepCodex does not restate response
percentages from early dose-escalation cohorts here, because dose-escalation response rates
are drawn from small, heterogeneous, dose-varying groups and are routinely misquoted as if
they were phase III results. Read the primary papers for those numbers in their proper
context. A companion briefing covers the
[PDC-versus-ADC comparison](/blog/peptide-drug-conjugate-solid-tumors) in more detail.

### The One Peptide-Targeting Approach That Is Approved

Peptide-mediated tumour targeting is not hypothetical. Radiolabelled somatostatin analogues
deliver a radioisotope rather than a cytotoxic drug, and they are established in
neuroendocrine tumour care: a randomised trial compared 177Lu-Dotatate plus long-acting
octreotide against high-dose long-acting octreotide in patients with midgut neuroendocrine
tumours [lutathera-netter]. The targeting peptide in that setting is a
[somatostatin analogue](/peptides/octreotide).

That is the strongest existing proof that a short peptide can direct a payload to a tumour
in humans. It is also a narrow proof — it depends on somatostatin receptor expression, which
most solid tumours do not have.

## What CBX-12 Is, and What Could Not Be Verified

CBX-12 is described in secondary coverage as a conjugate of exatecan to a pH-Low Insertion
Peptide, on the rationale that the extracellular environment of solid tumours is more acidic
than normal tissue and could serve as a targeting signal in place of a specific antigen.

PepCodex could not verify a clinical trial registration for CBX-12, could not locate a
publication reporting phase II results for it, and therefore reports no efficacy, safety, or
tolerability figures for it. The mechanism description above is the claim as it is made
elsewhere, not a finding this site can stand behind. Any percentage attributed to CBX-12
should be traced to a named registry record or paper before it is relied on.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

**Evidence level: low.**

- Published human data for PDCs is confined to first-in-human dose escalation and early
  expansion — single-arm, no comparator
- No PDC carrying a cytotoxic payload has regulatory approval as of 2026-08-17
- Survival benefit over standard care has not been demonstrated for any PDC
- Populations studied are heavily pretreated and not representative of earlier-line use

What is genuinely established is narrower and worth stating plainly: peptides can be
conjugated to a payload, given to humans, and taken through dose escalation to a published
result; and in the specific case of somatostatin receptor-positive neuroendocrine tumours,
peptide-directed delivery has randomised evidence behind it. Everything beyond that is
open. A [clinical trial](/glossary/clinical-trial) entry is not a result, and a
dose-escalation result is not an efficacy claim.

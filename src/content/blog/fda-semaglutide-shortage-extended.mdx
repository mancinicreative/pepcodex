**Correction notice:** An earlier version of this article, published 1 September 2025, reported that the FDA had *extended* the semaglutide shortage designation and that the shortage was expected to persist through at least mid-2026. That was wrong: the FDA had in fact determined the semaglutide injection shortage **resolved in February 2025** [fda-shortage-update]. Unverifiable supply-chain specifics — production-capacity growth figures, dose-strength availability details, and compounded-market share and pricing estimates — have also been removed. This article now stands as a dated record of the shortage period and its aftermath.

## The Shortage Period: What Happened

Demand for [semaglutide](/peptides/semaglutide) products (Ozempic, Wegovy) grew explosively after their approvals for type 2 diabetes and weight management — a demand surge rooted in the drug class's unprecedented efficacy for obesity pharmacotherapy [glp1-physiology]. Supply could not keep pace, and semaglutide injection products spent more than two years on the FDA's drug shortage list.

The shortage reflected genuine structural challenges in scaling peptide manufacturing:

**Complexity of production.** Peptide synthesis and sterile fill-finish operations require specialized equipment and expertise; capacity cannot be added as quickly as for small-molecule drugs.

**Quality requirements.** Pharmaceutical-grade peptide production demands stringent quality controls, and scaling while maintaining those standards takes time.

**Demand uncertainty.** The growth in GLP-1 prescriptions outran industry planning assumptions, and manufacturing investments carry long lead times.

## The Compounding Market the Shortage Enabled

The shortage designation carried a major regulatory consequence: under FDA rules, compounding pharmacies may prepare copies of commercially marketed drugs while those drugs are in shortage, provided certain conditions are met [fda-shortage-update]. A substantial compounded semaglutide market developed on that legal basis, driven by both supply gaps and the lower cost of compounded alternatives.

Compounded drugs are not FDA approved, and the agency does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before marketing. The FDA's standing safety communication on unapproved GLP-1 drugs documents the problems that accompanied the compounding boom — dosing errors, unapproved salt forms, cold-chain failures, and fraudulent labeling [fda-glp1-warning].

## What Happened Since — as of 17 August 2026

**The shortage ended.** The FDA determined the tirzepatide injection shortage resolved in December 2024 and the semaglutide injection shortage resolved in **February 2025**, confirming with the manufacturers that availability and capacity could meet projected national demand [fda-shortage-update]. [The shortage-resolution briefing](/blog/glp1-shortage-easing) covers the manufacturing investments behind that determination.

**The compounding basis fell away.** With semaglutide and [tirzepatide](/peptides/tirzepatide) off the shortage list, the shortage-based legal basis for routine compounding of these drugs no longer applies. Cost, rather than availability, became the main driver of remaining demand for compounded alternatives.

**Safety monitoring continued.** As of 31 May 2026, the FDA had received 990 adverse event reports associated with compounded semaglutide and more than 730 associated with compounded tirzepatide, figures the agency considers likely underreported [fda-glp1-warning]. [The compounded-semaglutide warning review](/blog/fda-compounded-semaglutide-warning) covers the quality concerns in detail.

## Why This Record Matters

The semaglutide shortage is a case study in what happens when a transformative drug class meets fixed manufacturing capacity: multi-year supply constraints, a parallel quasi-regulated market, and a slow regulatory unwinding once supply catches up. Those dynamics are likely to recur as new obesity and metabolic drugs launch into comparable demand.

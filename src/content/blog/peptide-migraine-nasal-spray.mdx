**Correction notice:** An earlier version of this briefing reported that a CGRP-blocking *peptide* nasal spray developed by "Neurotech Pharma" had entered Phase 2 trials for migraine prevention. No such company, compound, or trial could be located in any trial registry or the published literature, and those claims have been removed. What follows is the verified picture of intranasal CGRP blockade.

## The CGRP Pathway in Migraine

CGRP is a 37-amino acid peptide released during migraine attacks and central to migraine pathophysiology:

- **Vasodilation:** CGRP dilates meningeal arteries
- **Neurogenic inflammation:** promotes inflammatory signalling
- **Pain transmission:** enhances pain signal propagation

Blocking CGRP or its receptor has proven effective for both migraine prevention and acute treatment [cgrp-migraine-review].

Note the terminology trap that the peptide-adjacent internet gets wrong constantly: **CGRP is the peptide. Most drugs that block it are not.** The preventive agents are monoclonal antibodies (large proteins), and the acute agents — the "gepants" — are small molecules.

## What Is Actually Approved Intranasally

Zavegepant (marketed as Zavzpret) is a CGRP receptor antagonist delivered as a nasal spray and approved by the FDA for the **acute treatment** of migraine with or without aura in adults. It is a small molecule, not a peptide.

Its Phase 2/3 double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled dose-ranging trial found that 10 mg and 20 mg doses were effective for acute treatment with a favourable safety profile [zavegepant-trial]. In that trial, both doses produced pain freedom in more than 20% of patients and relief of the most bothersome symptom in more than 40%.

**Approved indication is acute treatment, not prevention.** Intranasal CGRP blockade is positioned as a fast-onset rescue option, particularly for patients with nausea or vomiting that makes oral dosing unreliable — not as a substitute for the preventive monoclonal antibodies.

## Current CGRP-Targeting Therapies

**Monoclonal antibodies (prevention), injectable:**
- Erenumab (Aimovig) — anti-CGRP receptor antibody
- Fremanezumab (Ajovy) — anti-CGRP antibody
- Galcanezumab (Emgality) — anti-CGRP antibody
- Eptinezumab (Vyepti) — anti-CGRP antibody, intravenous

**Gepants (acute treatment, and in some cases prevention), oral or intranasal:**
- Ubrogepant, rimegepant, atogepant — oral
- Zavegepant — intranasal

## Why Nasal Delivery Is Hard for Actual Peptides

The idea behind a peptide nasal spray is sound in principle — rapid onset, no needle, avoidance of first-pass metabolism. The obstacles are why so few exist:

- **Molecular size:** nasal epithelial permeability falls sharply as peptide size increases
- **Enzymatic degradation:** peptidases in the nasal mucosa degrade peptides before absorption
- **Limited volume:** the nasal cavity accepts only small volumes, capping deliverable dose
- **Variable absorption:** congestion, mucus, and technique all change the delivered dose

These are the reasons the successful intranasal migraine agent is a small molecule rather than a peptide.

## What This Means

For people with migraine, intranasal CGRP blockade is a real and approved option — for acute attacks. Any claim about a *preventive peptide* nasal spray should be checked against ClinicalTrials.gov before being taken seriously.

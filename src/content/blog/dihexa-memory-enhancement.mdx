Dihexa, a synthetic hexapeptide derived from angiotensin IV, is widely described in the nootropic community as a potent memory enhancer acting through hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) signalling. The animal literature does contain a genuine cognitive signal — but one of the papers most often cited for the mechanism has been retracted, and that materially changes how confident anyone should be about how dihexa works.

## What We Know

The clearest surviving in-vivo evidence comes from a 2021 study in APP/PS1 transgenic mice, a model of Alzheimer's disease. Orally administered dihexa restored spatial learning in the Morris water maze and tended to increase target-quadrant crossings relative to untreated transgenic controls [dihexa-memory-2025].

At the tissue level, dihexa raised synaptophysin expression and neuronal counts in the cerebral cortex, and reduced markers of neuroinflammation (IL-1β and TNF-α down, IL-10 up, with less astrocyte and microglial activation). The effects were traced to PI3K/AKT signalling: the PI3K inhibitor wortmannin reversed the benefit [dihexa-memory-2025].

### A retraction that matters

Much of the widely repeated account of dihexa — that it increases hippocampal dendritic spine density, drives functional synaptogenesis, and that these effects are abolished by c-Met blockade — traces to a 2014 paper in the *Journal of Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics* (Benoist et al., PMID 25187433). **That paper has been retracted over data-integrity concerns.** Claims resting on it should not be treated as established, and PepCodex does not cite it.

The broader proposition that the brain HGF/c-Met system is a plausible target for cognitive disorders, and that dihexa is orally active and brain-penetrant, is set out in review work from the same field [nootropic-peptides-review]. Hepatocyte growth factor itself is a well-characterised growth factor with functions extending well beyond the liver [hgf-cognition].

### Mechanism and Background

Dihexa was developed by researchers at Washington State University as part of efforts to understand how angiotensin IV enhances cognition. The hexapeptide sequence was identified as the minimal fragment required for cognitive effects, and subsequent modifications increased potency to picomolar concentrations.

The connection to HGF was unexpected. HGF is primarily known for its roles in liver regeneration and tissue repair, but it is also expressed in the brain where it promotes neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. The proposal that dihexa amplifies this signalling, potentially restoring function lost to aging or disease, is set out in review work by the group that developed the compound [nootropic-peptides-review] — but given the retraction described above, it should be read as a hypothesis awaiting independent replication rather than a settled mechanism.

Unlike many potential cognitive enhancers, dihexa is orally bioavailable, crossing the blood-brain barrier effectively after oral administration. This property makes it particularly attractive as a potential therapeutic, though it also facilitates unregulated use.

## What It Means

The surviving animal data keep HGF/c-Met on the list of candidate targets for cognitive disorders, but they no longer carry the weight they once appeared to. A cognitive benefit in one transgenic mouse model, mediated through PI3K/AKT, is a much narrower claim than the synaptogenesis story that circulated on the strength of the retracted work.

Translation to humans also remains uncertain. Many promising cognitive enhancers have failed to replicate animal findings in human trials. The complexity of human cognition and the limitations of rodent memory models create significant translational challenges.

Dihexa has gained attention in the nootropic community, where it is sold through gray-market suppliers for self-experimentation. This uncontrolled use raises safety concerns, as long-term effects in humans are unknown. HGF signaling has been implicated in certain cancers, creating theoretical risks that have not been evaluated in safety studies.

The lack of human clinical trials despite years of promising animal data reflects challenges in developing cognitive enhancement drugs. Regulatory pathways are unclear, pharmaceutical company interest has been limited, and the healthy-aging indication lacks clear endpoints.

## What's Next

Academic researchers are seeking funding for first-in-human studies, with a focus on older adults experiencing mild cognitive impairment. Such trials would establish basic safety and pharmacokinetics while gathering preliminary efficacy signals.

Pharmaceutical interest may increase as more data accumulates. The oral bioavailability and novel mechanism differentiate dihexa from other cognitive enhancement approaches, potentially attracting development partnerships.

Basic science continues to elucidate the HGF/c-Met pathway in the brain. Understanding which neuronal populations express c-Met, how signaling changes with aging, and how the pathway interacts with other cognitive systems could inform clinical development strategies.

For individuals interested in cognitive enhancement, the available evidence does not support dihexa use outside of controlled research. The favorable animal data must be weighed against unknown human safety, uncertain efficacy, and product quality concerns with unregulated sources.

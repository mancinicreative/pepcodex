A Phase 2 trial of MOTS-c, a mitochondrial-derived peptide, is now registered and recruiting in adults with prediabetes and overweight or obesity. That registration is the first point at which MOTS-c moves from preclinical research toward human testing — and it is worth separating what has been registered from what has been demonstrated.

## What Is MOTS-c?

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded within the 12S rRNA region of mitochondrial DNA. It was identified in 2015 and belongs to a class of mitochondrial-derived peptides (MDPs) that act as signalling molecules [cell-metabolism-motsc].

Key characteristics of MOTS-c:

- **Mitochondrial origin:** Unlike most peptides, which are encoded in nuclear DNA, MOTS-c is encoded in mitochondrial DNA
- **Systemic signalling:** MOTS-c acts beyond the cell that produces it, and transfers to the nucleus under metabolic stress to regulate gene expression [motsc-review]
- **Decline with age:** MOTS-c levels decrease with age [motsc-review]

## Preclinical Evidence

The founding study reported that MOTS-c targets skeletal muscle, inhibits the folate cycle and its tethered de novo purine biosynthesis, and thereby activates AMPK. In mice, MOTS-c prevented both age-dependent and diet-induced insulin resistance and obesity [cell-metabolism-motsc].

Review-level work since then has examined MOTS-c in the context of aging, cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders, including its effects on glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle [motsc-review].

Everything above is animal and cell work. It establishes a mechanism and a rationale — not a human effect.

## The Registered Human Trial

### Study Design

As of July 2026, one interventional MOTS-c trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov
(NCT07505745), *MOTS-c for Improving Insulin Sensitivity in Adults With Prediabetes and
Overweight/Obesity*:

- **Phase:** Phase 2
- **Status:** Recruiting
- **Planned enrollment:** 120 participants
- **Conditions studied:** Prediabetes, insulin resistance, overweight/obesity
- **Sponsor:** Hudson Biotech
- **Interventions:** MOTS-c (mitochondrial-derived peptide) versus placebo, given subcutaneously [motsc-trial]

No results have been published from this trial. Registration establishes that a study is planned
and underway — it is not evidence of efficacy, and nothing about MOTS-c's effects in humans should
be inferred from it.

We do not list dose cohorts, titration schedules, or eligibility thresholds. Those are matters for
the trial investigators and a treating physician, not a reference article.

## Scientific Rationale

### Why MOTS-c?

Several factors make MOTS-c an attractive therapeutic candidate:

1. **Endogenous peptide:** MOTS-c is naturally produced in humans, potentially reducing safety concerns
2. **Decline with age:** Lower levels correlate with aging and metabolic dysfunction
3. **Multiple targets:** MOTS-c affects metabolism, inflammation, and cellular stress responses
4. **Exercise mimetic:** May provide some exercise benefits to those unable to exercise adequately

### Mechanism of Action

The mechanism described in the founding study runs through one-carbon metabolism:

**Folate cycle inhibition → AMPK activation:**
- MOTS-c targets skeletal muscle and inhibits the folate cycle and its tethered de novo purine biosynthesis
- This leads to activation of AMP-activated protein kinase, a central metabolic regulator [cell-metabolism-motsc]

**Nuclear signalling:**
- Under metabolic stress, MOTS-c transfers to the nucleus, where it participates in regulating gene expression [motsc-review]

Broader roles for mitochondrial-derived peptides in aging biology have been reviewed [longevity-peptides-review], but the specific downstream pathways in humans have not been characterised.

## Challenges and Considerations

### Manufacturing

Producing MOTS-c for clinical use presents challenges:

- **Synthesis complexity:** 16 amino acids requires careful synthesis
- **Purity requirements:** Clinical-grade peptide needs >98% purity
- **Stability:** Peptide stability in formulation must be established
- **Cost:** Current production costs limit accessibility

### Delivery

Optimal delivery remains an open question. The registered trial uses subcutaneous injection; whether alternative routes are viable for a peptide of this size has not been established in humans.

## Context in Longevity Research

### Mitochondrial-Derived Peptides

MOTS-c is part of a broader class of mitochondrial-derived peptides:

- **Humanin:** The first discovered MDP, shows neuroprotective effects
- **SHLP peptides:** Six additional MDPs with various functions
- **Collective importance:** MDPs may explain mitochondrial-nuclear communication

### Comparison to Other Interventions

| Intervention | Mechanism | Status |

MOTS-c offers a distinct approach through directly replacing a declining endogenous factor.

## What Success Would Mean

### Scientific Implications

A positive result would:

- Provide the first human efficacy signal for a mitochondrial-derived peptide
- Support the "endocrine mitochondria" concept
- Open pathways for other MDP development
- Provide human pharmacokinetic and safety data, which do not currently exist

### Clinical Implications

If MOTS-c proves safe and effective:

- New approach to metabolic syndrome treatment
- Potential intervention for age-related functional decline
- Possible exercise mimetic for those unable to exercise
- Bridge therapy during lifestyle modification programs

## What This Means

The start of human testing for MOTS-c is a milestone in mitochondrial peptide research. After a decade of preclinical evidence, researchers will begin to learn whether MOTS-c produces metabolic benefits in people. Nothing in the preclinical record guarantees that it will, and no timeline to approval can responsibly be projected from a single recruiting trial.

For the field of longevity research, this trial represents an important test of whether restoring declining endogenous factors can reverse aspects of metabolic aging. The results will inform not only MOTS-c development but also broader strategies targeting mitochondrial function in aging.

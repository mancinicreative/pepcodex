## What a phase label describes

A trial phase describes the **main questions a study is designed to answer** during drug development. It does not report whether the study met its outcomes or whether a product is approved. FDA describes a typical progression from early human studies through larger Phase 3 studies, followed by a separate application review ([FDA clinical research](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research); [FDA drug review](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-4-fda-drug-review)). These terms apply to investigational drugs generally, including peptides studied as drugs.

**Evidence grade: high for the FDA definitions below.** A phase label is not an evidence grade for any particular peptide.

| Stage | Main question | What the label alone cannot tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preclinical | What do laboratory and animal studies suggest before human testing? | Whether the product is safe or beneficial in people. |
| Phase 1 | How does the investigational drug behave in people, and what early safety findings appear? | Whether it provides a clinical benefit. |
| Phase 2 | What preliminary effectiveness and common short-term safety findings appear in people with the studied condition? | Whether a benefit is confirmed in a larger population. |
| Phase 3 | What do expanded studies show about effectiveness and safety for a specified population? | Whether the results support approval or apply to everyone. |
| Phase 4 | What additional safety or effectiveness information appears after approval? | That all longer-term or uncommon risks are already known. |

The FDA's [clinical research overview](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research) describes the purposes of Phases 1–4. Preclinical work precedes human trials; Phase 4 is post-market research. These rows describe usual aims, not mandatory study sizes, durations, or a guaranteed sequence for every product.

## Phase 1: early human questions

Phase 1 commonly examines how a drug acts in people and its early side effects. Participants may be healthy volunteers or people with the condition, depending on the study. A Phase 1 result can inform later trial design; the label alone does not establish a treatment benefit ([FDA clinical research](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research)).

## Phase 2: preliminary effectiveness

Phase 2 studies examine preliminary effectiveness and further safety information in people with the condition being studied. Their findings help shape later research questions and Phase 3 protocols. “Phase 2” does not mean a benefit is settled or that the drug is ready for clinical use ([FDA clinical research](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research)).

## Phase 3: expanded evaluation

Phase 3 studies are designed to evaluate treatment benefit and adverse reactions in a specified population on a larger scale. Whether a particular study supports a claim depends on its actual results, comparator, outcomes, and limitations. The FDA then reviews the **full application**, including clinical data and other evidence, before deciding on approval ([FDA clinical research](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research); [FDA drug review](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-4-fda-drug-review)).

## Phase 4: questions after approval

Phase 4 research takes place after approval and can add information about safety and effectiveness. Approval does not end evidence gathering ([FDA clinical research](https://www.fda.gov/patients/drug-development-process/step-3-clinical-research)).

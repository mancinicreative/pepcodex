## Read the setting before the conclusion

The [PARACHUTE paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6298200/) appeared in the BMJ's 2018 Christmas collection. Its participants jumped from stationary aircraft near the ground. This makes it a useful editorial example of a question to bring to peptide papers: does the study setting match the claim being made?

This is an exercise in reading a report, not a peptide study or guidance on parachute use. Keep the [blank reading worksheet](/guide/how-to-read-peptide-research) beside it.

## A filled-in reading note

The following details come from the [paper's Methods, Results and Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6298200/).

| Field | What the report says |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Parachute versus empty backpack for preventing death or major trauma after an aircraft jump. |
| Participants | 92 screened; 23 randomized. Table 1 lists 11 in the parachute group and 12 in control; a Results paragraph reverses those arm counts. |
| Setting | Enrolled participants jumped from a mean height of 0.6 metres; aircraft speed was zero. |
| Design and analysis | Randomized, unblinded; intention-to-treat analysis. |
| Primary outcome | Death or major traumatic injury (Injury Severity Score above 15), within five minutes of impact. |
| Observed outcome | Zero primary-outcome events in either group. |

## What can be calculated, and what remains open

The arm-count discrepancy remains unresolved in the records we inspected. Using Table 1's arm counts, our arithmetic is **0/11 minus 0/12 = 0 percentage points**. Reversing the denominators would still give zero here because neither group had an event. This is the observed absolute difference, not proof of equivalence. The unadjusted risk ratio would require dividing zero by zero and is undefined; we do not replace it with 1 or call it a 0% relative reduction.

The authors reported no confidence interval for the binary outcome contrast. We have not calculated a replacement interval. Their reporting choice does not establish that every statistical method is incapable of producing an interval.

The missing high-altitude setting is central to our reading. This experiment does not answer the question of parachute benefit during high-altitude flight. More generally, a statistically non-significant result alone does not demonstrate absence of effect; see [Cochrane Chapter 15, sections 15.2–15.3](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15).

**Evidence grade: not assigned.** This is a reading note on one report, not a body-of-evidence assessment. We do not infer a high, moderate, low or very-low certainty grade for peptide outcomes from it.

## Follow the correction trail

The [journal correction dated December 18, 2018](https://www.bmj.com/content/363/bmj.k5343) amended the Methods sentence defining statistical significance to a P value below 0.05. This correction addresses the significance threshold, not the conflicting arm counts. Keep the correction with the original paper; its DOI is a different identifier.

For your own note, keep three entries: the original claim, the passage that changes its interpretation, and your revised summary. Our revised summary here is: **the observed result belongs to the near-ground setting actually studied.** It supplies no result about a peptide.

## A name is the start of the question

"Peptide" describes a molecule built from amino acids joined by peptide bonds. "Protein" is commonly used for longer amino-acid chains, but there is no universal residue count at which one term becomes the other. The [IUPAC-IUB nomenclature](https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/AminoAcid/A1113.html) says authors differ on that boundary. Our [terminology guide](/guide/peptide-vs-protein) explains the basic terms; this article asks what those labels can actually tell you about a medicine.

**The short answer:** neither label proves that a product works, is safe, has a particular delivery route, or has regulatory approval. Those questions require evidence about the **specific molecule and formulation**. Reviews of peptide drug discovery describe many different structures and development strategies; they are context for evaluating a claim, not evidence that every product called a peptide has the same effect ([Muttenthaler et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33536635/); [Lau and Dunn, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28720325/)).

## Four checks before accepting a claim

| Check | Ask for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | The exact molecule, including any named modification | A class label cannot identify which substance was studied. |
| Formulation | The product and route described in its source or label | Delivery depends on the formulated product, not the word "peptide." |
| Evidence | The actual human study and its measured outcome, if a clinical benefit is claimed | A mechanism or a review of a drug class is not a trial of the product in question. |
| Regulatory status | The jurisdiction and the official product label or authorization | Approval of one named product does not approve a class or another formulation. |

These are reading checks, not a clinical scoring system. The reviews above describe the breadth of peptide drug development; they do not provide a patient-level safety or efficacy grade for an unnamed product.

## A delivery example: semaglutide

The same active ingredient illustrates why molecule class does not determine route. Official DailyMed labeling identifies [oral semaglutide tablets](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=27f15fac-7d98-4114-a2ec-92494a91da98) and a separate [semaglutide injection](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=adec4fd2-6858-4c99-91d4-531f5f2a2d79). The route belongs to each labeled formulation. It would be wrong to infer from the word "peptide" alone that a product must be injected, can be taken orally, or can be substituted for another formulation.

This example establishes a distinction about formulation only. It does not compare clinical outcomes or give instructions for using either product.

## What to conclude

Use "peptide" and "protein" to understand a molecule's general chemistry. For a medical claim, move to the named product, the original study, its measured outcomes, and the applicable label. A marketing phrase or residue-count shortcut cannot replace those records.

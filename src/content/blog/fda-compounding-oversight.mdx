The FDA has closed the regulatory window that allowed compounding pharmacies to produce large volumes of [semaglutide](/peptides/semaglutide) and [tirzepatide](/peptides/tirzepatide). With both drugs removed from the shortage list and absent from the 503B bulks list, the statutory conditions that supported shortage-based compounding no longer hold.

## Background: The Compounding Situation

During the FDA's official shortage designation for semaglutide and tirzepatide, compounding pharmacies operated under regulatory flexibility that allowed them to produce versions of these medications. This created a significant market, with compounded products often sold well below brand-name prices and hundreds of thousands of patients using them.

## What FDA Actually Changed

### Shortage resolution and enforcement deadlines

FDA resolved the tirzepatide injection shortage in December 2024 and the semaglutide injection shortage in February 2025, then set phased deadlines for compounders to wind down [fda-compounding-guidance]:

- **Tirzepatide, section 503A** (state-licensed pharmacies and physicians): enforcement discretion ended following the 5 March 2025 district court ruling in *Outsourcing Facilities Association v. FDA*
- **Tirzepatide, section 503B** (outsourcing facilities): enforcement discretion ran until 19 March 2025
- **Semaglutide, section 503A:** enforcement discretion ended 22 April 2025
- **Semaglutide, section 503B:** enforcement discretion ran until 22 May 2025

FDA noted it could still act on violations of other requirements, including findings that a product is of substandard quality or otherwise unsafe.

### The "essentially a copy" limit

FDA reminded 503A compounders that they may not regularly, or in inordinate amounts, compound drugs that are essentially copies of a commercially available product — including combinations such as semaglutide plus vitamin B12 where the route of administration matches and strengths fall within 10% of the commercial products. FDA stated it does not intend to act against a compounder filling four or fewer prescriptions of such a product in a calendar month [fda-compounding-guidance].

### The 503B bulks list

Outsourcing facilities may compound from a bulk drug substance only if that substance appears on the 503B bulks list, or if the drug is on FDA's shortage list at the time of compounding. **Tirzepatide and semaglutide appear on neither** [fda-compounding-guidance].

## Quality Concerns in the Background

FDA's separate standing communication on unapproved GLP-1 drugs documents dosing errors leading to hospitalisation, use of unapproved semaglutide salt forms, products arriving without adequate refrigeration, fraudulent labelling naming pharmacies that did not compound the product, and hundreds of adverse event reports for both compounded semaglutide and compounded tirzepatide. Independent case-report literature has raised similar safety concerns [nejm-compounding-safety].

The peer-reviewed literature since has moved beyond anecdote in three specific directions.

**Impurities and immunogenicity.** A 2026 analysis in *Pharmaceutical Research* examined impurities and potential immunogenicity associated with follow-on and compounded GLP-1 receptor agonists [pmid-42533250]. Immunogenicity is the concern that distinguishes peptides from small molecules: an impurity profile that would be inert in a tablet can provoke an antibody response against an injected peptide.

**Formulation differences.** A 2026 editorial in *Annals of Pharmacotherapy* makes the point plainly in its title — compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide products use **unique formulations** whose efficacy and safety are largely unknown [pmid-41689811]. A compounded product is not a generic; it is a different formulation that has not been through bioequivalence testing.

**Administration errors.** A case series of administration errors with compounded semaglutide reported to a poison control centre documents the practical failure mode [pmid-37392810]: products supplied in units and concentrations that differ from the branded pen, in the hands of patients accustomed to the pen.

The American College of Clinical Pharmacy's endocrine and metabolism practice and research network has published a position on compounded incretins in clinical practice covering this ground [pmid-41176849].

## Industry Response

### Telehealth Platforms

Companies that built business models around compounded GLP-1 agonists face significant challenges:

- Several have announced pivots to brand-name medication prescribing
- Some have ceased offering compounded GLP-1 products entirely
- FDA has warned telehealth companies for marketing unapproved drugs such as retatrutide, including direct-to-consumer marketing [fda-compounding-guidance]

### Brand Manufacturers

Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk, makers of the brand-name products, have welcomed the stricter oversight. Both companies have increased manufacturing capacity and expressed concerns about patient safety with compounded products.

## Patient Impact

### Access Concerns

The change will likely reduce availability of low-cost compounded GLP-1 products:

- **Price barrier:** Brand-name medications cost $1,000-1,500/month without insurance
- **Insurance gaps:** Many insurance plans still don't cover GLP-1 agonists for obesity
- **Geographic disparities:** Compounding pharmacies served patients in areas with limited specialty pharmacy access

### Safety Benefits

However, moving patients onto FDA-approved products should improve safety:

- Consistent, validated potency
- Reduced contamination and cold-chain risk
- Established beyond-use dating and stability data
- Mandatory adverse event reporting

## Legal Considerations

### 503A vs 503B Pharmacies

The change has different implications for different pharmacy types:

**503A pharmacies** (traditional compounding): Must compound pursuant to valid prescriptions for individual patients, and are subject to the "essentially a copy" restriction described above.

**503B outsourcing facilities**: Can compound without patient-specific prescriptions but must register with FDA, comply with current good manufacturing practices (cGMP), and — when compounding from bulk substances — rely on either the 503B bulks list or an active shortage listing. Neither applies to semaglutide or tirzepatide.

### Enforcement Position

The phased enforcement-discretion periods described above have all expired [fda-compounding-guidance]. FDA has since taken enforcement action against telehealth companies marketing unapproved GLP-1 drugs, API distributors supplying compounders, and sellers labelling GLP-1 products "for research purposes."

## What Happens Next

### Litigation

The wind-down has been contested. District courts denied preliminary injunctions sought by the Outsourcing Facilities Association in both the tirzepatide and semaglutide cases, in March and April 2025 respectively [fda-compounding-guidance].

### Market Adjustments

The peptide compounding market will likely consolidate:

- Smaller pharmacies may exit peptide compounding
- Larger 503B facilities may gain market share
- Some patients may transition to brand-name products
- Others may seek unregulated sources (concerning from safety perspective)

## What This Means

The FDA's action represents a significant shift in peptide compounding oversight. While it addresses legitimate quality concerns, it also raises access questions for patients who had come to depend on lower-cost compounded options.

The practical situation for people currently receiving compounded GLP-1 products is that the legal basis for those products has changed underneath them, the formulations they were receiving were never bioequivalence-tested against the branded products [pmid-41689811], and the alternatives differ sharply in cost. Those are questions for a prescribing clinician and a licensed pharmacist, not for a web page.

How the market adapts — and whether displaced patients move to approved products or to unregulated sources — is the open question, and it is the one with the clearest safety consequences.

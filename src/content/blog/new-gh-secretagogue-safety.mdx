Growth hormone secretagogues (GHS) are frequently presented as a cleaner alternative to growth hormone itself: stimulate the body's own pulsatile release rather than injecting exogenous hormone. The mechanism is real. The reason the class has not become a broad therapy is a structural problem that no compound in public use has solved.

## How the Class Works

Growth hormone secretagogues stimulate the body's own production and release of GH rather than supplying hormone from outside [gh-physiology-update]. They do this by activating the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR1a) — the ghrelin receptor — on pituitary somatotrophs, which triggers GH release while leaving normal negative feedback in place.

That is the appeal: pulsatile release with intact feedback regulation, rather than a flat exogenous exposure.

## The Selectivity Problem

The difficulty is that GHSR1a is not a dedicated growth-hormone switch. It is the ghrelin receptor, and ghrelin's physiological roles include appetite stimulation and effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Activating the receptor to obtain GH release tends to activate its other consequences too.

This is why the widely discussed compounds in this class carry the profiles they do. Appetite stimulation, cortisol and prolactin effects, and glucose or insulin-sensitivity changes are recurring features of GHSR agonism rather than incidental impurities of any one molecule. A compound that separates GH release from these effects would have to achieve biased signalling at the same receptor — which is a hard pharmacology problem, not a formulation tweak.

Claims that a particular research peptide is "selective" and free of appetite, cortisol or glucose effects should be checked against published human data, which for most compounds in this space does not exist.

## What Is Actually Approved

Two drugs acting at the growth hormone secretagogue receptor have regulatory approval — **macimorelin** and **anamorelin** — and their molecular recognition by GHSR has been characterised [ghsr-approved-drugs].

Their indications are instructive. Neither is approved as a general growth-hormone therapy or an anti-aging intervention. Macimorelin is used diagnostically, and anamorelin is used in the cancer anorexia-cachexia setting, where the appetite effect of ghrelin receptor agonism is the therapeutic goal rather than a side effect.

In other words, the one place where GHSR agonism has clearly succeeded clinically is where the "off-target" appetite effect is the point.

## Where Research Is Going

Work on novel secretagogues continues, generally aimed at defined disease settings rather than at broad GH replacement. A recent preclinical example evaluated JMV2894, a growth hormone secretagogue, in the D2-mdx mouse model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy [ghs-preclinical-jmv2894].

This is representative of the current state of the field: novel GHS compounds are being characterised in animal models for specific indications. Preclinical findings in a mouse model do not establish a human safety or efficacy profile.

## Secretagogue Versus GH Replacement

The theoretical case for each is worth stating plainly.

**Secretagogue advantages:** maintains pulsatile release, preserves feedback regulation, potentially lower immunogenicity.

**GH replacement advantages:** predictable and controllable dosing, extensive clinical experience, characterised long-term safety, and approval for defined indications.

The second column is where the evidence currently sits. Growth hormone deficiency has established, approved treatment; secretagogues do not have an approved role in it.

## Open Questions

The questions that would need answering before this class broadened are specific:

- Can GH release be pharmacologically separated from appetite, cortisol and glucose effects at the same receptor?
- Does the GH-releasing effect persist over extended treatment, or does tachyphylaxis develop?
- Do biomarker changes such as IGF-1 elevation translate into clinical benefit?
- What are the long-term safety implications, including with respect to malignancy risk, of sustained GH-axis stimulation?

None of these has been resolved. Until they are, the honest summary is that growth hormone secretagogues are a well-understood mechanism with two narrow approved uses and no established role in GH deficiency or aging.

Novo Nordisk announced in 2025 that the EVOKE and EVOKE Plus clinical trials evaluating [semaglutide](/peptides/semaglutide) in early Alzheimer's disease failed to meet their primary endpoints. The results were a significant setback for the hypothesis that GLP-1 receptor agonists might slow neurodegenerative disease progression, and they highlight the continued difficulty of developing effective Alzheimer's treatments.

**Verification status, as of 17 August 2026:** both trial registrations are verified — EVOKE (NCT04777396) and EVOKE Plus (NCT04777409) are each listed on ClinicalTrials.gov as completed phase 3 trials enrolling 1,840 participants [evoke-registration] [evoke-plus-registration]. The outcome described here reflects the sponsor's topline announcement; no primary peer-reviewed EVOKE publication is in PepCodex's verified source registry as of this date, and specific effect sizes are therefore not reported in this article.

## What We Know

The EVOKE program consisted of two phase 3 trials, each enrolling 1,840 participants with early Alzheimer's disease — mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid pathology [evoke-registration] [evoke-plus-registration]. Participants received once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (the same molecule used in Ozempic and Wegovy) or placebo for approximately two years.

### Trial Design and Rationale

The decision to test semaglutide in Alzheimer's was based on multiple lines of evidence suggesting potential neuroprotective effects of GLP-1 receptor activation:

**Observational data:** large database studies had reported that patients taking GLP-1 agonists for diabetes appeared to have lower rates of dementia diagnosis compared to those taking other diabetes medications. While subject to confounding, these observations generated hypothesis-testing interest.

**Preclinical evidence:** animal studies suggested GLP-1 agonists could reduce neuroinflammation, protect neurons from various insults, and enhance synaptic plasticity in some models.

**Mechanistic plausibility:** GLP-1 receptors are expressed in the brain, including regions affected by Alzheimer's disease, and the metabolic improvements associated with GLP-1 agonists — effects on insulin signaling, inflammation, and vascular function — all have theoretical relevance to neurodegeneration.

**The liraglutide precedent:** an earlier, smaller trial of [liraglutide](/peptides/liraglutide), an older GLP-1 agonist, showed signals of potential benefit on imaging and biomarker measures, encouraging further investigation.

### Primary Outcome Results

Both EVOKE trials evaluated change from baseline in the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), a validated measure of cognitive and functional decline — the same primary endpoint used in recent successful trials of anti-amyloid antibodies.

Per the sponsor's announcement, neither EVOKE nor EVOKE Plus showed a statistically significant slowing of CDR-SB decline compared to placebo, although numerical trends favored semaglutide on some measures. When primary endpoints fail, secondary endpoint signals must be interpreted with great caution due to multiple-comparison issues and the risk of selective reporting.

## What We Don't Know

The negative results raise as many questions as they answer.

**Why didn't it work?** Several possibilities exist: the observational associations may have been confounded; preclinical models may not translate to human Alzheimer's disease; the exposure, duration, or timing of treatment may have been suboptimal; or the trial population — early Alzheimer's with confirmed amyloid — may not be the right target.

**Are all GLP-1 agonists equivalent?** Semaglutide has limited brain penetration compared to some other GLP-1 agonists. Whether a more brain-penetrant compound might perform differently remains an open question.

**Prevention versus treatment.** The EVOKE trials enrolled patients who already had diagnosable Alzheimer's disease. Whether GLP-1 agonists might have preventive effects in at-risk individuals without established disease cannot be determined from these results.

**Subgroup responses.** As of 17 August 2026, no primary EVOKE publication is in the verified registry, so subgroup analyses, biomarker data, and exact effect sizes cannot yet be independently examined.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

Two large, completed phase 3 trials that failed to meet their primary endpoints constitute strong evidence against this specific application — semaglutide, at the tested exposure and duration, in early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease. What the trials do not exclude: benefits with other GLP-1 agonists, benefits in other populations or disease stages, or smaller effects the trials were not powered to detect.

Until the full data appear in a peer-reviewed journal, the outcome rests on the sponsor's topline reporting rather than independently examinable results — one reason this article grades the evidence moderate rather than high.

## What's Next

**Full data publication.** A peer-reviewed EVOKE publication had not appeared in PepCodex's verified source registry as of 17 August 2026. When it does, the scientific community can examine subgroup analyses and biomarker data directly.

**Other programs.** Research into incretin-based drugs and cognition continues, including work on [tirzepatide](/peptides/tirzepatide) and brain-penetrant GLP-1 analogs. The EVOKE results lower expectations for the class in symptomatic Alzheimer's disease. For the separate observational literature on GLP-1 use and dementia incidence, see [the dementia-risk study review](/blog/glp1-dementia-risk-study).

**Clinical practice implications.** For patients and physicians who hoped GLP-1 agonists might serve the dual purposes of metabolic management and dementia prevention, these results indicate that cognitive protection should not be considered an established benefit. The metabolic and cardiovascular benefits of these medications remain well supported.

The EVOKE results are a reminder that biological plausibility, observational associations, and preclinical success do not guarantee clinical efficacy. They also represent the scientific process working as intended: rigorous trials providing clear answers about therapeutic hypotheses.

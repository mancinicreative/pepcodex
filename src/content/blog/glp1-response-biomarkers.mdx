Response to GLP-1 receptor agonist medications varies substantially between individuals. Review articles in precision obesity medicine and incretin pharmacogenetics describe candidate genetic and multi-omic predictors of that variability — but they also make clear that none of these markers are validated or available for clinical use.

## The Response Variability Problem

While GLP-1 agonists like [semaglutide](/peptides/semaglutide) and [tirzepatide](/peptides/tirzepatide) produce substantial average weight loss in trials, individual results range from very large reductions to essentially none. Clinicians currently cannot predict, before starting treatment, where a given patient will fall on that range.

This creates practical problems:

- Patients may spend months on therapy that does not work for them
- Insurance coverage often requires documented failure before alternatives are approved
- Healthcare costs accumulate without benefit for non-responders
- Patient frustration and treatment dropout increase

Obesity is a heterogeneous condition, and reviewers have argued that treatment selection should eventually incorporate genetic, physiological, and behavioural characteristics rather than being applied uniformly [precision-obesity-review].

## What the Genetics Research Has Found

A review of the pharmacogenetics of incretin-based therapies reports that missense variants of the *GLP1R* gene — which encodes the GLP-1 receptor itself — have been associated with the effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors on glycaemic control and on weight. The review identifies several additional genes with apparent pharmacogenetic relevance to this drug class [pharmacogenomics-glp1].

Two caveats are essential to how these findings should be read:

- The associations are reported from research cohorts, not from prospective trials that assigned treatment based on genotype.
- The review's own conclusion is that these findings are **not yet applied clinically**, and represent early progress toward precision medicine rather than a usable test [pharmacogenomics-glp1].

## The Broader Multi-Omics Argument

Beyond single-gene pharmacogenetics, precision obesity medicine proposals argue for combining multi-omic technologies — genomics, metabolomics and related approaches — with physiological and behavioural phenotyping to guide mechanism-based treatment selection [precision-obesity-review].

This is a research programme, not current practice. Reviewers describe it as a direction for the field rather than a set of validated tools.

## Why This Is Not Yet Clinically Usable

Several gaps separate the current literature from a test a clinician could order:

**No validated predictive panel.** There is no established, prospectively validated combination of markers that reliably sorts patients into responders and non-responders before treatment.

**Testing infrastructure.** Genetic testing is not routine in obesity practice, protein biomarker panels generally require specialised laboratories, and metabolomic profiling remains a research technique.

**Population generalisability.** Pharmacogenetic associations frequently fail to replicate across ancestries and cohorts, so findings from one study population cannot be assumed to transfer.

**Drug-specific effects.** Evidence relating to one incretin drug does not automatically apply to another; the dual and triple agonists now in use and in development have different receptor targets.

**Coverage questions.** Whether payers would cover predictive testing — or begin requiring it — is entirely unresolved.

## What This Means

Biomarker research is a genuinely active area, and the underlying premise is sound: obesity has heterogeneous biology, and the same drug will not suit every patient equally. But at present the evidence consists of association findings and review-level argument, not a deployable clinical tool.

For patients, the practical takeaway is narrower and still useful: a poor response to one medication reflects the mismatch between that mechanism and that individual's biology, not a personal failure. Treatment selection today remains empirical, guided by clinical response rather than by any pre-treatment test — see [the weight-loss peptide evidence comparison](/blog/best-peptide-for-weight-loss-2026) for how the approved options differ on trial evidence.

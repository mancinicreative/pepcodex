The prolonged shortage of GLP-1 receptor agonist medications has formally ended. The FDA has determined that the shortages of both tirzepatide and semaglutide injection products are resolved, following several years of large manufacturing investment by Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk.

## What We Know

The FDA determined that the tirzepatide injection shortage was resolved in a declaratory order issued in December 2024, and made the same determination for semaglutide injection products in February 2025. In each case the agency stated that it had confirmed with the manufacturer that stated product availability and manufacturing capacity can meet present and projected national demand [fda-shortage-update].

Resolving a shortage listing has a direct downstream consequence: once FDA considers a drug commercially available, compounders are substantially restricted from producing copies of it. The agency set defined transition windows for state-licensed pharmacies and outsourcing facilities to wind down compounding of each drug [fda-shortage-update].

Novo Nordisk's capacity build-out included a $4.1 billion investment announced in June 2024 in a second fill-and-finish facility at its Clayton, North Carolina campus. That project adds roughly 1.4 million square feet of aseptic manufacturing and finished-production space and about 1,000 jobs, with completion expected between 2027 and 2029 [novo-manufacturing]. Separately, the company committed more than 42 billion Danish kroner from 2023 to expanding its Kalundborg, Denmark site, with the majority directed at active pharmaceutical ingredient capacity and construction completing in phases from the end of 2025 through 2029.

Eli Lilly has similarly expanded tirzepatide production. In reporting its second-quarter 2025 results, the company attributed its volume growth in part to having expanded manufacturing capacity to meet increasing demand [lilly-supply].

Note that a resolved shortage listing is a national supply determination, not a guarantee for any individual pharmacy. FDA has acknowledged that patients and prescribers may still encounter intermittent localised supply disruptions as product moves through the distribution chain.

### Causes of the Shortage

The shortage resulted from demand that far exceeded manufacturers' production planning. When semaglutide first gained widespread attention for weight loss, neither the companies nor industry analysts anticipated the surge in prescriptions that followed.

Peptide manufacturing is complex and time-intensive, with production cycles measured in months rather than weeks. Facilities must meet stringent quality standards, and expanding capacity requires significant capital investment and regulatory approval. The lag between recognising unmet demand and delivering additional supply proved substantial.

The shortage was also filled in part by compounding pharmacies producing copies of GLP-1 medications, some of which faced quality concerns. With the shortage listings resolved, the regulatory basis for that compounding has largely closed.

## What It Means

For patients who were unable to access GLP-1 medications or faced frequent switches between dose strengths, improved supply offers stability. Consistent access to prescribed doses supports treatment outcomes and reduces the disruption that characterised the shortage period.

Healthcare providers can prescribe with greater confidence that patients will be able to fill prescriptions. The shortage created difficult situations where providers had to help patients manage supply gaps or switch between available alternatives.

The pharmaceutical industry has learned hard lessons about demand forecasting and manufacturing flexibility. Future launches in this class will likely incorporate more aggressive capacity building ahead of approval.

Insurance coverage dynamics may shift as supply normalises. Some payers relaxed prior authorisation requirements or coverage restrictions during the shortage; whether those accommodations continue remains uncertain.

## What's Next

Both manufacturers continue to expand capacity beyond current levels in anticipation of ongoing demand growth, and the obesity treatment market is widely expected to expand further as additional indications are approved.

Competition will intensify as generic liraglutide reaches more markets and as new oral formulations and alternative GLP-1 medications launch. This competition should further improve access and may moderate pricing.

International supply varies, with some countries still experiencing constraints. Manufacturers prioritise markets differently, creating geographic disparities in access that may persist longer than in the United States.

Patients who had been using compounded versions should discuss transition plans with their healthcare providers, since the enforcement windows that permitted that compounding have closed.

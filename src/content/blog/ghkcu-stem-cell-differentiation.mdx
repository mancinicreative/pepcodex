GHK-Cu, the copper complex of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, is routinely described as activating stem cells and driving tissue regeneration. There is real research behind that description. It is entirely preclinical, and one of its most-repeated details — that the copper is what does the work — is contradicted by the study most often cited for the stem cell claim.

The full compound record is in the [GHK-Cu dossier](/peptides/ghk-cu); the copper-free form has its own page, [GHK](/peptides/ghk).

## The Stem Cell Finding, and Its Twist

The study most directly on point is titled, unambiguously, *Stem cell recovering effect of **copper-free** GHK in skin* [pmid-23019153].

Its authors set the scene by noting their own prior finding that copper-GHK promotes the survival of basal stem cells in skin, then ask what the copper-free peptide does — a question they describe as poorly investigated. Working in cultured normal human keratinocytes and skin-equivalent models, they found that **GHK without copper increased keratinocyte proliferation**, and that adding GHK during skin-equivalent culture affected the basal cell population [pmid-23019153].

That matters because the standard summary runs the other way: that GHK is merely a copper carrier and the metal is the active principle. The published record supports a more careful statement — both forms have documented activity in skin models, and separating their contributions is an open question rather than a settled one.

## What the Mechanistic Literature Describes

The tripeptide binds Cu(II) with an affinity similar to the copper transport site on albumin, forming the GHK-Cu complex [pmid-18644225]. A review of tissue remodelling attributes a broad set of actions to the two molecules together: chemoattraction of repair cells including macrophages, mast cells and capillary cells; and anti-inflammatory effects including suppression of free radicals and of thromboxane formation, alongside increased superoxide dismutase and vessel vasodilation [pmid-18644225].

A later review revisited GHK-Cu in the light of gene-expression data [pmid-29986520], which is where much of the peptide's contemporary reputation comes from — broad transcriptional effects are impressive to read about and difficult to translate into a specific clinical claim.

**A note on how to read this.** Broad mechanism lists are the weakest form of strong-sounding evidence. A molecule that appears to touch many pathways in cell culture has not thereby been shown to help any particular condition in a person.

## The Animal Data

Two in-vivo results give the clearest picture of what has actually been demonstrated.

**Scald wounds in mice.** GHK-Cu encapsulated in liposomes promoted proliferation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells by 33.1%, shifted the distribution of cells toward G1 and away from G2, and raised expression of VEGF, FGF-2, CDK4 and cyclin D1. In a mouse scald model, angiogenesis in burned skin increased with treatment [pmid-28370978]. Note that the peptide had to be **liposome-encapsulated** to achieve this — a delivery-system result as much as a peptide result.

**Skeletal muscle in a COPD model.** A 2023 study in *Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle* measured plasma GHK in patients with COPD (n=9) against age-paired healthy subjects (n=11), then showed GHK-Cu rescued cigarette-smoking-induced skeletal muscle dysfunction through a sirtuin 1-dependent pathway [pmid-36905132]. The human component here is a nine-person biomarker comparison, not a treatment study.

## Historical Context

GHK was identified in human plasma in the 1970s, was observed to decline with age, and has since been incorporated widely into cosmetic products. That commercial history is why the peptide has a large, uneven literature: a great deal of cell-culture and formulation work, and very little in the way of controlled human trials.

A 2025 review examining topically applied GHK as an anti-wrinkle peptide is candid about this, framing the field in terms of advantages, **problems**, and prospects rather than settled benefit [pmid-39963574].

## What This Does Not Show

- **No human clinical trial** of GHK-Cu for wound healing or skin aging is cited here, because the searches behind this page did not surface one meeting the site's evidence bar.
- **Concentrations used in cell culture** may not be reachable through topical application; the skin barrier limits penetration, and the mouse result required liposomal delivery [pmid-28370978].
- **Gene-expression breadth is not efficacy.** A transcriptional signature indicates that something is happening, not that it helps.
- **Plasma decline with age is an observation, not a rationale for replacement.** Many things decline with age; the inference that restoring them is beneficial requires its own evidence.

## What Would Move This Forward

A randomised controlled trial of a defined topical formulation, with a pre-specified endpoint — wound closure rate, or a validated skin measure — and enough participants to detect a moderate effect. Delivery-system work, since the animal evidence suggests formulation is limiting. And a direct comparison of GHK against GHK-Cu in the same model, given that the copper-free peptide has documented activity of its own [pmid-23019153].

Until then, GHK-Cu is best described as a mechanistically rich preclinical compound with a large commercial presence and a thin clinical record — not as a demonstrated stem cell therapy.

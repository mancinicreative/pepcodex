SS-31 — known in clinical development as elamipretide — is a mitochondria-targeting tetrapeptide with an unusually clean mechanistic story and an unusually disappointing cardiac trial record. Both halves matter, and most coverage reports only the first.

The compound now carries an FDA approval. It is not for heart failure.

## What Is Actually Approved

Elamipretide was approved by the FDA as **Forzinity**, NDA 215244, sponsored by Stealth BioTherapeutics, with the original application approved **19 September 2025** for **Barth syndrome** — a rare X-linked disorder of cardiolipin remodelling [forzinity-nda215244]. That indication is narrow and specific. Every other use, heart failure included, remains investigational.

The distinction matters because "FDA-approved peptide for mitochondrial disease" and "approved treatment for heart failure" are separated by a decade of trials that did not read out the way the mechanism predicted.

## The Mechanism

SS-31 is a cell-permeable tetrapeptide (D-Arg-dimethylTyr-Lys-Phe-NH2, under 700 daltons) that concentrates in the inner mitochondrial membrane and binds cardiolipin, the phospholipid that organises the electron transport chain. Binding stabilises the respiratory complexes and limits cardiolipin peroxidation during oxidative stress.

Mechanistic reviews describe elamipretide-cardiolipin interactions that alter mitochondrial membrane electrostatic potential and the assembly of cardiolipin-dependent proteins, with downstream effects on ATP synthesis and oxidative stress [elamipretide-mechanism]. Cardiolipin abnormalities are documented in failing human hearts, where peroxidation and remodelling track with disease severity [hf-mitochondria]. The target is real and the biology is not speculative — which is exactly why the clinical result is instructive.

For the full compound record, including scoring and the current regulatory entry, see the [SS-31 dossier](/peptides/ss-31).

## The Preclinical Case

Across animal models, elamipretide has been reported to raise left ventricular ejection fraction in dog models of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and to limit left ventricular remodelling in rats [ss31-cardiac-study]. The structural rationale is that the peptide preserves mitochondrial cristae architecture in cardiomyocytes under stress, where untreated tissue shows swelling and cristae disruption.

Work has continued. A 2026 study in *Circulation: Heart Failure* reported that elamipretide improved skeletal muscle performance in a rat model of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [pmid-42290373] — relevant because exercise intolerance in HFpEF is driven substantially by peripheral, not cardiac, mitochondrial impairment.

## The Clinical Record

Here the picture changes.

| Trial | Phase | Indication | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| PROGRESS-HF ([NCT02788747](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02788747)) | 2 | Stable heart failure; primary outcome was change in left ventricular end-systolic volume | Completed, results posted 2020 |
| [NCT03098797](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03098797) (TAZPOWER) | 2/3 | Barth syndrome; 6-minute walk distance and fatigue score | Completed, results posted 2024 — the basis of the approved indication |
| [NCT05162768](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05162768) | 3 | Primary mitochondrial myopathy | Completed |
| [NCT06373731](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06373731) (ReNEW) | 3 | Dry age-related macular degeneration | Active, not recruiting |

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of mitochondria-targeted agents in heart failure pooled 19 trials across six agents in 2,603 patients [pmid-42440741]. Across all agents, treatment improved LVEF (SMD 0.53, 95% CI 0.42–0.65) and reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.82) and heart-failure hospitalisation (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.42–0.85), with low certainty throughout.

Elamipretide's own contribution was two trials and 71 subjects. It was **not** associated with a significant LVEF improvement versus baseline, and in meta-regression it was the least effective of the agents evaluated — L-carnitine outperformed both elamipretide and coenzyme Q10 [pmid-42440741].

That is the honest headline for the cardiac indication, and it is the opposite of what the preclinical literature predicts.

## What It Means

Three readings survive the data.

**The mechanism is sound; the cardiac translation is not established.** Cardiolipin stabilisation is measurable and reproducible. Converting it into a change in ventricular function in humans has not been demonstrated in an adequately powered trial.

**Sample size is a real confound, not an excuse.** Seventy-one subjects across two trials cannot exclude a moderate benefit. "Least effective in meta-regression" and "insufficiently studied" are compatible statements, and the meta-analysis authors graded the whole evidence base as low certainty.

**The approved indication tells you where the signal was strongest.** Barth syndrome is a monogenic cardiolipin disorder — the one population where the drug's target is unambiguously the disease mechanism. Approval there, after failure in general heart failure, is consistent with a compound that works when mitochondrial dysfunction is the primary defect rather than one contributor among many.

## What's Next

Elamipretide development continues outside cardiology: primary mitochondrial myopathy, dry age-related macular degeneration through the ongoing ReNEW phase 3, and a phase 4 Barth syndrome commitment following approval. Trials in HFpEF specifically have been discussed as a better-matched population than HFrEF, on the reasoning that peripheral mitochondrial impairment is closer to the peptide's mechanism — a hypothesis the 2026 rat data support and no human trial has yet tested.

For readers weighing this compound, the useful summary is short: a real drug, a real approval, a real mechanism, and no demonstrated benefit in heart failure.

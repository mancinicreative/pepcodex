KPV is the Lys-Pro-Val tripeptide at the C-terminus of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone. This structural relationship does not make the parent hormone's results evidence for administered KPV.

### Direct experimental evidence

Dalmasso and colleagues tested KPV in human-derived intestinal and immune cell lines and in two mouse colitis models. Their experiments associated PepT1-mediated cellular uptake with reduced NF-kappaB/MAPK signaling and inflammatory readouts; oral KPV reduced colitis measures in mice. This is **cell and animal evidence**, not a human pharmacokinetic or efficacy study. A separate later study reported reduced colonic PEPT1 expression during inflammation in studied samples and mice, so the transport story should not be generalized beyond the experimental setting. [PMID:18061177](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18061177/) · [PMID:24583477](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24583477/)

Other preclinical work examined receptor-independent anti-inflammatory effects. It does not establish that KPV treats inflammatory bowel disease, skin disease or any other condition in people, nor does it prove the absence of pigmentation effects in humans. Studies of KdPT, a modified tripeptide, should also remain distinct from KPV. [PMID:12750433](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12750433/) · [KdPT study, PMID:21741932](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21741932/)

### Human evidence and safety boundary

No controlled KPV treatment trial or human drug-exposure dataset was identified. FDA states that it has identified **no human exposure data** for KPV drug products by any route and lacks information needed to determine whether administration would cause harm. Interactions and combination effects with other peptides are unknown. [FDA compounding safety review](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/certain-bulk-drug-substances-use-compounding-may-present-significant-safety-risks)

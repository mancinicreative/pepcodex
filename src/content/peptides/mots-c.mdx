MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded in the mitochondrial 12S rRNA region. The original 2015 paper found effects on folate and purine metabolism, AMPK signaling, and insulin-related outcomes in mice; it did not establish a human treatment effect. [Lee et al., 2015, PMID 25738459](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25738459/)

### Human observations and administered-peptide research

Human work has measured the body's own MOTS-c. A study of 77 participants found lower serum levels with more severe obstructive sleep apnea, but did not administer the peptide. A separate diabetes paper compared circulating levels in people with type 2 diabetes while its treatment experiments were in mouse islets and mice. These are distinct populations and neither provides treatment efficacy data. [Luo et al., 2025, PMID 40538389](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40538389/) · [Kong et al., 2025, PMID 40855115](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40855115/)

A 2021 study combined human exercise-associated MOTS-c measurements with mouse treatment experiments. A 2026 muscle study used human physiological sampling but assigned administered-peptide effects to mouse experiments; it did not find an arterio-venous MOTS-c difference during exercise in people. Human biomarker changes do not show that an injection mimics exercise. [Reynolds et al., 2021, PMID 33473109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33473109/) · [Gudiksen et al., 2026, PMID 41520850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41520850/)

ClinicalTrials.gov registers a native MOTS-c intervention for insulin sensitivity in adults with prediabetes and overweight or obesity. Its registration is a study plan, not an efficacy or safety result. The completed CB4211 trial tested a **distinct MOTS-c analog**, so its human exposure does not establish native MOTS-c outcomes. A kidney-transplant anesthesia study measures MOTS-c levels as a biomarker; it does **not** administer MOTS-c. [NCT07505745](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07505745) · [NCT03998514](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03998514) · [NCT07678073](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07678073)

### Preclinical scope and safety

A 2026 paper identified antimicrobial and immune effects in cell systems and a mouse infection model. Its experiments with human monocytes were laboratory studies, not clinical treatment of infection. This expands the mechanistic questions around MOTS-c without changing its human efficacy grade. [Rice et al., 2026, PMID 42611943](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42611943/)

A separate 2026 study administered MOTS-c to C26 tumor-bearing mice and tested it in cultured muscle cells. It found partial preservation of skeletal-muscle mass in that mouse cachexia model, without preventing overall body-weight or fat loss. It had no human treatment arm, did not test treatment after established cachexia, and does not establish preservation of muscle or exercise capacity in people. [Jamnick et al., 2026, PMID 42266945](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42266945/)

FDA lists MOTS-c among withdrawn bulk-drug nominations with potential immunogenicity and peptide-characterization risks. Its page says it has not identified human exposure data for **compounded MOTS-c products** and lacks sufficient safety information to know whether they would cause harm. The registered trial does not supply a published safety result. [FDA compounding safety page](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/certain-bulk-drug-substances-use-compounding-may-present-significant-safety-risks)

Metabolic, exercise, infection, and longevity treatment claims remain unestablished for people. This dossier is a research summary, not medical advice or self-treatment guidance.

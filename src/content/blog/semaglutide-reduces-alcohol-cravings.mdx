Alcohol use disorder affects approximately 400 million people globally, yet treatment options remain limited and relapse rates high [who-alcohol]. An unexpected observation in patients using GLP-1 medications for diabetes or obesity sparked formal investigation: many report spontaneously drinking less. The first randomized trial built specifically to test that observation with [semaglutide](/peptides/semaglutide) has now completed, and a phase 3 program has begun.

## What We Know

**The STAR-T phase 2 trial.** Semaglutide Therapy for Alcohol Reduction - Tulsa (STAR-T, NCT05891587) is a completed, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial that enrolled 80 adults with alcohol use disorder — a trial designed for alcohol use disorder itself, not for weight loss or diabetes [star-t-registration]. Its reported findings pointed to reductions in craving-related and drinking-related outcome measures with semaglutide relative to placebo. *Verification note: as of 17 August 2026, the trial's primary results publication is not yet in PepCodex's verified source registry, so this article describes the trial record and direction of findings without reporting specific effect sizes.*

**The preclinical foundation.** The neurobiological basis for a GLP-1 effect on alcohol behavior is well documented in animals. GLP-1 receptors are found not only in the gut and pancreas but also in brain regions involved in reward processing. The GLP-1 analogue exendin-4 attenuates alcohol-mediated behaviors in rodents, including alcohol intake and alcohol-seeking [exendin-alcohol-rodents]. More recent work shows that semaglutide, [tirzepatide](/peptides/tirzepatide), and retatrutide all attenuate the interoceptive (internally sensed) effects of alcohol in male and female rats — evidence that the effect extends across the incretin class [incretin-alcohol-interoception].

The proposed mechanism involves modulation of dopamine signaling in reward circuits: GLP-1 receptor activation appears to reduce the rewarding properties of alcohol without causing the aversive reactions associated with some older alcohol treatments.

**Converging observations.** Real-world reports from patients using semaglutide for metabolic conditions align with the trial direction — reduced interest in alcohol, often described as alcohol simply becoming less appealing rather than requiring willpower to avoid. Observational reports of this kind are hypothesis-generating, not proof.

## What We Don't Know

The phase 2 trial was small (80 participants) and of moderate duration. Larger trials are needed to confirm efficacy, and several questions remain open:

- **Durability:** whether any effect persists after discontinuing the medication. Alcohol use disorder is a chronic, relapsing condition, and whether semaglutide produces lasting change or requires ongoing treatment matters for any future clinical use.
- **Patient selection:** alcohol use disorder exists on a spectrum, and individuals with different drinking patterns, mental health conditions, or treatment histories may respond differently.
- **Other substances:** how GLP-1 receptor agonists affect broader addiction patterns is under investigation.
- **Human mechanism:** the reward-circuit mechanism is established in animals; it has not been fully validated in humans.

## What's Next

The verified trial pipeline has grown considerably:

- **CRAVE (NCT07218354)** — a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled **phase 3** trial of semaglutide in U.S. veterans with alcohol use disorder, recruiting 622 participants as of 17 August 2026 [crave-phase3]. This is the confirmatory-stage test the phase 2 signal needed.
- **Class-effect trials** — registered studies are testing other incretin agents in alcohol use disorder, which will help distinguish semaglutide-specific effects from a class effect. For the mechanism research behind this, see [the GLP-1 alcohol-research review](/blog/glp1-alcohol-cravings-research).

If phase 3 results confirm the phase 2 direction, a regulatory submission for an alcohol use disorder indication could follow — which would represent the first major new medication class for alcohol treatment in decades.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

The current evidence is best characterized as **suggestive**. One small completed phase 2 trial provides controlled data in an alcohol use disorder population, moving beyond anecdote and observation — but phase 2 trials establish proof of concept, not efficacy, and the primary publication is not yet in the verified registry. The consistent preclinical literature [exendin-alcohol-rodents] [incretin-alcohol-interoception] strengthens biological plausibility, and multiple converging lines of evidence point the same direction. The field has, however, seen promising early signals fail to replicate in larger trials before.

Semaglutide is not approved for alcohol treatment. For individuals with alcohol use disorder, these findings are noteworthy but not actionable; those interested in research participation can find enrolling trials on ClinicalTrials.gov.

## Start with the claim

Write down the claim you want to check. Then identify the exact molecule, who or what was studied, the comparison, the outcome, and the follow-up period. Keep those details beside the claim as you read.

## Copy this reading note

Use a plain document or notebook. No account or submission is needed for the worksheet.

```text
Claim I am checking:
Paper title and link:
Publication date; correction notice; date I read it:
Material available: abstract / full paper / supplement / protocol

Question: What comparison is being investigated?
Population: Who or what was studied? Who was excluded?
Setting: Where did the study happen?
Intervention and comparator:
Design: How were groups formed? Who was blinded?
Outcome: What exactly was measured, in which units?
Timepoint: When was that outcome measured?
Analysis: Who was included, and how were missing data handled?
Result: Each group's result and denominator, then the comparison.
Absolute result and relative result, if applicable:
Uncertainty: Reported interval and important unmeasured outcomes.
Applicability: How does this setting differ from my question?
Funding, author interests and funder's role:
Other relevant studies or conflicting findings to check:

My bounded summary:
What this paper does not establish:
Open questions; next source to read:
Passage/table/figure supporting each factual note:
```

For a randomized trial, compare your note with the report's eligibility, outcome, analysis, participant-flow and limitation sections. [CONSORT's checklist](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11995449/#tbl1) asks authors to report these details. A missing detail remains a question; do not fill it from a trial's name or phase.

## Questions for a randomized trial

Use these reading prompts with the original report:

1. **What was planned?** Check the registration, protocol, and primary outcome against the reported results.
2. **How was the sample size justified?** Read the assumptions behind it, not just the participant count.
3. **Who contributed results?** Look for the numbers assigned to each group and included in the analysis.
4. **How uncertain is the estimate?** Read the effect estimate alongside its confidence interval.
5. **How were harms collected?** Check the definitions, assessment methods, and follow-up.
6. **Who funded and conducted the work?** Read the funder's role and the authors' conflict disclosures alongside the methods.

These prompts draw on [CONSORT 2025, Table 1 and Item 16a](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/350055/1/350055.pdf). Sample-size planning depends on the study question and design assumptions; a fixed cutoff such as 50 participants is not a substitute for that appraisal. CONSORT is reporting guidance, not a quality score or an automatic pass/fail test.

## Keep the number attached to its meaning

Record the measure as the authors report it. A difference in event percentages is an absolute difference; a risk ratio compares the risks by division. Do not silently exchange these measures. For continuous measurements, retain the units and whether the result is a final value or change from baseline. See [Cochrane Chapter 15, sections 15.4–15.5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15).

A confidence interval expresses statistical uncertainty under the analysis assumptions. Its width depends on more than participant count, including outcome variability or the number of events. A result that does not meet a statistical-significance threshold does not by itself establish no effect. Read the estimate and interval together. [Cochrane Chapter 15, section 15.3](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15).

## Look beyond the design label

Name the study design, then ask whether it addresses the particular claim. For a laboratory or animal experiment, record that setting explicitly instead of rewriting the result as a human outcome.

For an assessment of a body of evidence, look for an explanation of bias, precision, consistency, and relevance. The [GRADE Working Group's criteria](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/) call for considering these factors and assessing evidence for specified outcomes. A design label alone does not provide that assessment.

**Evidence grade for this worksheet: not assigned.** This methods guide does not assess a body of peptide evidence. If you record a grade from another review, also record its outcome, population, rationale and date; do not turn it into a blanket score for a compound.

## Keep a short reading note

Record the source link, the claim, the measured outcome, and the limitations you identified. Mark unanswered questions as unanswered. When reading a second paper, check whether its population and outcome match before treating it as confirmation of the same claim.

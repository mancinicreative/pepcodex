**Correction notice:** An earlier version of this article described a dose-response
meta-analysis of 38 randomised trials and over 1,800 participants, reporting an
inverted U-shaped dosing curve with peak effects at a specific intranasal dose. That
analysis could not be located in the published literature, and its figures — including
the dosing claims — have been removed. This article now reports what the verifiable
pooled analyses measured and found. It has also been reframed: the verified literature
does not support any "optimal dosing" conclusion, and PepCodex does not publish dosing
guidance.

Intranasal oxytocin is one of the most-trialled interventions for autism spectrum
disorder, and one of the most misdescribed. The pooled evidence is worth reading for
what it actually measured: social and repetitive-behaviour outcomes across many small
randomised trials — not a validated treatment effect, and not a dosing curve.

## What the Pooled Analyses Measured

Oxytocin is a nine-amino-acid [neuropeptide](/glossary/neuropeptide) produced in the
hypothalamus, with roles in social bonding and affiliative behaviour. The hypothesis
that supplementing it might improve social functioning in autism has driven well over a
decade of small randomised trials, which pooled analyses have since tried to make sense
of [oxytocin-review].

A preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis examined oxytocin administration
effects on two outcome domains in autism [oxytocin-review]:

- **Social outcomes:** a small but statistically significant pooled effect
  (d = 0.22, p &lt; 0.001)
- **Routinized (repetitive) behaviours:** no significant effect (d = 0.14, p = 0.22)
- **The authors' own caveat:** the summary effects "might be inflated due to
  publication bias"

A separate systematic review and meta-analysis focused on core symptoms in children
pooled five randomised controlled trials totalling 486 participants
[oxytocin-autism-children]. Outcomes were measured on standard instruments — the
Social Responsiveness Scale and the Repetitive Behavior Scale–Revised. One trial showed
statistically significant improvement on both; the pooled analysis suggested a possible
effect on narrow interests and repetitive stereotyped behaviour. The authors concluded
that oxytocin's therapeutic value **is not fully established** and called for larger,
more robust trials.

Note that the two analyses do not agree on which symptom domain, if any, responds.
Instability of that kind across pooled analyses is itself evidence about how fragile
the underlying finding is.

## Where the Dosing Narrative Came From — and Why It Fails

Claims about an "optimal dose" of oxytocin for autism circulate widely. The verified
pooled literature cannot support them, for three reasons.

**The trials are small and heterogeneous.** Most enrolled fewer than 50 participants,
with varying ages, administration schedules, treatment durations, and outcome measures
[oxytocin-autism-children]. Pooling can estimate an average effect across that
variation; it cannot cleanly isolate a dose-response relationship from confounded
study-level differences.

**The pooled effect itself is in doubt.** When the authors of the largest preregistered
analysis warn that their d = 0.22 may be inflated by publication bias
[oxytocin-review], any secondary claim built on top of that effect — including claims
about which regimen produces it — inherits the same doubt.

**Regimen optimisation presumes an established effect.** Fine-tuning administration
parameters is a question that comes after a treatment effect is demonstrated. For
oxytocin in autism, that prior question remains open.

## The Biomarker Question

One recurring hypothesis is that oxytocin might help the subset of autistic people with
lower endogenous oxytocin. A preregistered meta-analysis of 18 studies and 1,422
participants examined endogenous oxytocin levels in autistic versus neurotypical
individuals [asd-biomarker-study]:

- **Children:** autistic children had significantly lower levels (g = −0.60, p = 0.006)
- **Adolescents and adults:** no significant difference
- **Sex:** the difference appeared primarily in studies of male participants

That is a real but narrow finding — concentrated in male children and absent in older
groups — and the authors state that measurement methods are too heterogeneous for
oxytocin levels to serve as a diagnostic or treatment-selection tool today.

## What This Means

The honest summary of the pooled evidence: a small social-behaviour effect that the
analysts themselves flag as possibly inflated, no reliable effect on repetitive
behaviours, disagreement between analyses about which domain responds, and no validated
way to select who might benefit. No oxytocin product is approved for autism, and the
current evidence does not establish that one should be.

## What is GHK-Cu?

GHK-Cu (glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine bound to copper) is a naturally occurring tripeptide found in human plasma, saliva, and urine. It was identified through its ability to bind copper and modulate tissue repair, and it is now one of the most widely used peptides in cosmetic skincare [pickart-2008]. Plasma levels of GHK decline substantially with age, which is one reason it attracted anti-aging research interest [pickart-2012].

The full evidence-graded record lives in the [GHK-Cu dossier](/peptides/ghk-cu).

## Key Facts

| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Natural** | Yes — found in human plasma |
| **Primary use** | Topical skincare and cosmetic formulations |
| **Evidence level** | Moderate for topical cosmetic use; largely preclinical elsewhere |
| **Regulatory status** | Legal as a cosmetic ingredient; not an approved drug |

## How Does It Work?

The published mechanism research — much of it cell-culture and animal work — points to several actions:

- **Collagen stimulation.** The original 1988 finding: GHK-Cu stimulated collagen synthesis in cultured human fibroblasts [maquart-1988]
- **Tissue remodeling.** GHK modulates matrix metalloproteinases and their inhibitors, the enzymes that break down and rebuild skin structure [pickart-2008]
- **Gene expression.** Profiling studies report GHK resets the expression of a large number of genes involved in repair and inflammation toward a healthier pattern [pickart-2018]
- **Antioxidant activity.** Copper delivery and oxidative-stress protection are proposed contributors [pickart-2012]
- **Stem cell support.** Even copper-free GHK increased markers of skin stem cell activity in laboratory studies [choi-2012]

## What Is the Evidence For?

### Skin and cosmetic use — the strongest case

GHK-Cu's best evidence is topical. Reviews of the cosmetic literature describe facial studies reporting improved skin firmness, elasticity, and reduced fine lines and photodamage with GHK-Cu creams [pickart-2015]. Most of these trials are small, industry-run, and published as cosmetic rather than clinical research — real signals, but a lower evidence tier than drug trials.

### Wound healing — mostly preclinical

Wound repair is where GHK-Cu research began, and animal models support faster closure — for example, GHK incorporated into collagen matrices accelerated healing in diabetic wounds in rats [arul-2007]. Controlled human wound-healing trials remain limited; the site classes this indication as preclinical-to-early evidence.

### Everything else — preliminary

Gene-expression work has proposed roles in lung injury, nerve regeneration, and other tissue repair contexts [pickart-2018], but these are hypothesis-generating laboratory findings, not demonstrated clinical benefits.

## What the Evidence Does Not Show

As of this article's last verification (2026-08-17), no regulatory agency has approved GHK-Cu as a drug for any condition, and no large randomized clinical trial of injected GHK-Cu appears in the verified literature. Systemic use sits well outside the published evidence base. The honest summary: good cell and animal data, credible small cosmetic trials, and a gap where definitive human trials would be.
